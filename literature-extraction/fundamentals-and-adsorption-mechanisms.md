## 1. What the topic is about

The presentation concerns the removal of antibiotic compounds from water-based systems. The selected papers discuss antibiotic residues in surface water, groundwater, rivers, lakes, wastewater-treatment effluents, hospital and pharmaceutical discharges, agricultural water, aquaculture and livestock-related wastewater. They describe antibiotics as contaminants of concern because residues can persist, affect aquatic organisms and microbial communities, and contribute to the selection or spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) and antibiotic-resistance genes (ARGs).

**Selected-source support**
- REF-02, pp. 4679–4680: antibiotic use, incomplete digestion/metabolism, detection in water and wastewater, hospital/household and pharmaceutical/industrial sources; structural classes and environmental distribution.
- REF-04, pp. 1–2: hospital effluents, industrial production waste, animal manure and irrigated agricultural land; detection in surface water, groundwater, seawater and soil; ARB/ARG context.
- REF-01, pp. 27082–27083: tetracycline structure/speciation, reported aqueous matrices, persistence and resistance context.
- REF-12, pp. 1–2: OTC/CIP in rivers, lakes, sewage effluent and livestock wastewater; weak biodegradability and persistence as described by the authors.
- REF-11, p. 2: CIP, clarithromycin and trimethoprim are selected as environmentally relevant antibiotic examples; the article discusses wastewater pathways and retention/release.
- REF-01, PDF p. 4 / journal p. 27084: the review states that primary and secondary treatment of residual antibiotic wastewater was insufficient to remove 100% of TC; this supports the need for advanced/tertiary treatment in that TC context.
- REF-04, PDF p. 2: the review states that conventional biological wastewater treatments are unable to remove antibiotic residues efficiently; treat this as a review-level limitation, not a universal treatment-performance estimate.

**Teaching qualification:** Avoid saying that every antibiotic is persistent in exactly the same way or that every conventional treatment plant has one universal removal percentage. The selected papers cover different compounds, matrices and treatment conditions. Present persistence and treatment limitations as concerns documented for the compounds and settings described by each source.

## 2. Antibiotics: definition and classification

For this presentation, introduce antibiotics as compounds used to prevent or treat bacterial infections. The papers emphasise their therapeutic use in humans and animals, alongside veterinary, livestock, aquaculture and other agricultural uses.

The selected sources use more than one classification scheme. REF-02 distinguishes bacteriostatic and bactericidal action and also lists structural/chemical classes. REF-05 lists major structure-based groups. REF-01 gives more detailed tetracycline subclasses and explains that tetracyclines differ by natural, semi-synthetic and synthetic preparation.

## 3. What is an aqueous medium?

**Operational teaching definition:** An aqueous medium is a liquid system in which water is the solvent or continuous liquid phase. In the selected studies it may be a prepared antibiotic solution, synthetic wastewater, or a real environmental/wastewater sample.

This is a plain-language chemistry definition for the presentation; the inspected papers use terms such as “aqueous solution”, “water”, “wastewater”, “surface water” and “aqueous matrix” but do not provide a standalone formal definition of “aqueous medium”.

Do not treat deionised laboratory water, synthetic wastewater and real wastewater as interchangeable. The matrix can contain dissolved ions, natural organic matter and other constituents that compete for adsorption sites or alter solution chemistry. REF-04 discusses natural-organic-matter competition in carbon adsorption; REF-02 identifies competing ions as a relevant factor; REF-11 examines three antibiotics in contact with different by-products and flow-chamber release conditions.

## 4. Adsorption terminology

The selected papers use the following terms throughout their experimental descriptions:

- **Adsorbate:** the dissolved species being captured; in this project, the antibiotic molecule or antibiotic species in the aqueous phase.
- **Adsorbent:** the solid material providing the surface, pores and/or active sites; examples in the collection include activated carbon, biochar, clays, magnetic composites and waste-derived by-products.
- **Adsorption:** accumulation or retention of molecules at an interface, particularly at the surface or accessible pore surfaces of a solid in contact with water.
- **Absorption:** transfer of a substance into the bulk of another phase, rather than its accumulation at an interface.
- **Sorption:** a broader term sometimes used when adsorption and absorption are not distinguished.
- **Biosorption:** REF-11 describes this in relation to sorbents derived from biological material, including microorganisms, plant biomass, agricultural residues and marine residues. It contrasts this material-origin use with adsorption, where the sorbent origin is not specified (REF-11, p. 2).

**Source boundary for the distinction:** REF-11 explicitly distinguishes adsorption and biosorption by the origin of the sorbent. The inspected selected-paper passages do not give a dedicated formal comparison of adsorption versus absorption. The adsorption/absorption distinction above is therefore a standard working chemistry explanation supplied for teaching, not a definition quoted or specifically established by one of the 15 papers. It should be presented as terminology, not as a finding from the review corpus.

### Adsorption sequence described in the biomass review

REF-02 describes the principal steps in the process for biomass-based adsorbents (PDF p. 9; journal p. 4686):

1. Movement of adsorbate molecules from the bulk matrix toward the adsorbent.
2. Diffusion through the liquid film surrounding the adsorbent particle.
3. Movement of adsorbate molecules toward accessible active sites.
4. Interaction between the adsorbate and adsorbent surface.

The review states that the adsorption mechanism depends on the final interaction step. The source-faithful sequence for the diagram is therefore: **bulk water → movement toward the adsorbent → diffusion through the surrounding liquid film → movement toward accessible active sites → adsorbate–adsorbent interaction**.

The review's Figure 3 (PDF p. 16; journal p. 4693) separately illustrates possible interactions/retention modes for antibiotics on biomass-based material, including pore filling, electrostatic interaction, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interaction and surface complexation. Pore filling may be shown as one possible mode, but it should not be inserted as a mandatory additional transport stage. The text on PDF p. 9 does not identify pore diffusion as a separate universal step.

**Wording caution:** The source text has a likely typographical phrase “adsorbent–adsorbent interactions” in the four-step passage. The surrounding context is about adsorbate molecules reaching active sites, and Figure 3 depicts antibiotic molecules interacting with biomass-based material. The slide therefore uses the chemically meaningful phrasing “adsorbate–adsorbent interaction” as an editorial clarification, not as a verbatim correction attributed to the authors.

### Processing and engineering are additional descriptors

These are not separate feedstock-origin families; they describe what was done to a base material:

- **Carbonisation / pyrolysis:** cotton husk is converted to biochar by pyrolysis under nitrogen (REF-12, PDF p. 3); sawdust is converted to biochar before ferrite deposition (REF-13, PDF p. 3); brewing bagasse is pyrolysed under argon (REF-14, PDF p. 3); and oxytetracycline fermentation residue is pyrolysed under nitrogen (REF-15, PDF p. 3).
- **Activation:** the bagasse-derived biochar is chemically activated with K2CO3 (REF-14, PDF p. 3); fermentation-residue biochar is activated with KOH during one-step pyrolysis (REF-15, PDF p. 3).
- **Composite formation / functionalisation:** cotton-husk biochar is combined with γ-Fe2O3 and ball milling to form Fe2O3@BMBC (REF-12, PDF pp. 2–3); NiFe2O4 is grown hydrothermally on sawdust-derived biochar to form NFO/BC (REF-13, PDF p. 3).

**Do not double-count feedstock and product:** Brewing bagasse and fermentation residue are feedstocks in these studies; the adsorbents used are their derived biochars (with activation). Cotton husk is also the feedstock for both biochar and the later magnetic composite. These should not appear as unrelated parallel material families.

**Terminology guardrail:** Use each paper's actual material label. Raw biochar, activated biochar and activated carbon are related carbonaceous materials, but the labels are not interchangeable without checking the preparation route and the terminology used by the source. REF-04 discusses the distinctions among AC, BC and hydrochar and describes activation as an important processing step (PDF pp. 6–7).


## 6. Adsorption mechanisms discussed in the selected literature

These are proposed or reported contributing interactions; they are not universal mechanisms that occur in every adsorbent–antibiotic pair.

### 6.1 Electrostatic interactions

Attraction or repulsion can occur between charged antibiotic species and charged adsorbent surfaces. The sign and magnitude depend on the antibiotic's acid–base speciation and the surface charge under the solution conditions.

- REF-04 identifies electrostatic interaction as a primary mechanism for activated-carbon/antibiotic interactions and also discusses hydrophobic, π–π, electron-donor–acceptor and hydrogen-bond interactions (PDF p. 7).
- REF-05 explains the negative basal charge of clay minerals, pH-dependent edge charge and the role of solution pH in antibiotic speciation and adsorption (PDF pp. 5–7).
- REF-01 gives tetracycline pKa values of 3.3, 7.7 and 9.7 and describes cationic, zwitterionic and anionic forms over different pH regions (PDF p. 2).
- REF-12 reports electrostatic interactions as one contributor to OTC/CIP adsorption on its magnetic cotton-husk biochar (PDF p. 1).

### 6.2 Hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen bonding may involve suitable donor/acceptor groups on the antibiotic and functional groups on the adsorbent surface. The selected sources describe oxygen-containing and nitrogen-containing surface groups as relevant examples.

- REF-04 lists –COOH, –OH and –NH2 groups and identifies hydrogen bonding among the interactions discussed for carbon-based adsorbents (PDF p. 7).
- REF-13's graphical abstract (PDF p. 2) illustrates hydrogen bonding between tetracycline and the NiFe2O4/biochar composite.
- REF-12 reports hydroxyl, carbonyl and Fe–O groups in the magnetic cotton-husk biochar and identifies H-bonding among the proposed contributing interactions (PDF p. 1).

### 6.3 π–π interactions and π–π electron-donor–acceptor interactions

These interactions are discussed for aromatic antibiotic structures and carbonaceous surfaces that contain conjugated/aromatic domains.

- REF-04 lists π–π interactions and π–π electron-donor–acceptor interactions among antibiotic–carbon interactions (PDF p. 7).
- REF-02 lists π–π electron-donor–acceptor interactions in its biomass/biochar discussion (PDF p. 9; journal p. 4686).
- REF-13's graphical abstract (PDF p. 2) depicts π–π interaction for tetracycline on NiFe2O4/biochar.
- REF-12 reports π–π conjugation as a contributing interaction for OTC/CIP adsorption (PDF p. 1).

### 6.4 Hydrophobic interactions / hydrophobic partitioning

The selected reviews describe hydrophobic interaction as one possible route for antibiotic association with carbonaceous or biochar materials. REF-14 also lists hydrophobic partition among mechanisms reported for pharmaceutical adsorption. Its importance depends on both the pollutant and adsorbent chemistry; do not assume that every antibiotic behaves as hydrophobic.

**Source:** REF-04, PDF p. 7; REF-02, PDF p. 9; REF-14, PDF p. 2.

### 6.5 Pore filling

Pore filling refers to antibiotic molecules entering and occupying accessible pores in a porous adsorbent. Pore size distribution and accessibility therefore matter, not only the headline BET surface-area value.

- REF-04 discusses pore structure as important for carbon-based adsorbents and includes pore filling in its TC/biochar discussion (PDF pp. 6–7).
- REF-14 lists pore filling among reported pharmaceutical adsorption mechanisms (PDF p. 2).
- REF-13's graphical abstract (PDF p. 2) depicts pore filling.
- REF-02 describes the importance of porosity and pore structure in biochar/activated carbon (PDF p. 9; journal p. 4686).

### 6.6 Material-specific ion exchange and complexation

These labels describe different interactions and should not be merged into one universal mechanism:

- **Cation exchange in clay minerals:** REF-05 explains that clay-layer charge is compensated by exchangeable cations. In swelling clays, cation exchange can occur on internal and external basal surfaces; particle size and interlayer distance can influence the process (PDF p. 6). The review also describes pH-dependent edge charge and short-range binding at clay edges (PDF pp. 5–6).
- **Metal complexation on iron-containing adsorbents:** REF-12 reports Fe–O complexation among the proposed contributing interactions for OTC/CIP on Fe₂O₃@BMBC (PDF p. 1; its FTIR/XPS and surface-chemistry discussion is on PDF pp. 5–6).
- **Cation bridging and other broad categories:** REF-14's introduction lists ion exchange, metal complexing and cation bridging among mechanisms reported in the wider pharmaceutical-adsorption literature (PDF p. 2). This is a broad literature list, not evidence that all these mechanisms occur in its own ciprofloxacin–activated-bagasse-biochar system.
- **Specific NiFe₂O₄/biochar example:** REF-13's graphical abstract depicts electrostatic force, hydrogen bonding, π–π interaction and pore filling for tetracycline on NFO/BC (PDF p. 2). Treat that figure as the authors' proposed conceptual mechanism for that material–antibiotic pair, not as a universal mechanism map.

**Mechanistic caution:** Do not infer a specific surface complex or chemical bond merely because a kinetic or isotherm model fits. Where a paper proposes a mechanism from pH behaviour, spectroscopy, surface chemistry or other observations, describe it as a “proposed” or “contributing” interaction unless the source establishes stronger evidence. A graphical abstract is the authors' conceptual representation and should be labelled accordingly.

## 7. Why pH and antibiotic speciation are central

pH can affect two things at once:

1. The ionic form of the antibiotic (protonated, zwitterionic or deprotonated species).
2. The protonation state and surface charge of the adsorbent.

The result can be electrostatic attraction or repulsion, and may alter the overall adsorption behaviour.

### Tetracycline example from REF-01

REF-01 reports three pKa values: approximately 3.3, 7.7 and 9.7. It describes cationic TC below pH 3.3, zwitterionic TC between pH 3.3 and 7.7, and anionic TC above pH 7.7. The figure on PDF p. 3 (Figure 1) shows the TC structure and its pH-dependent species distribution (PDF pp. 2–3; journal pp. 27082–27083).

### Oxytetracycline–kaolinite example from REF-05

REF-05 reports pKa values of 3.57, 7.49 and 9.88 for oxytetracycline and describes four ionic fractions as pH changes. It discusses how the pH-dependent charge of kaolinite and OTC affects attraction/repulsion, with the cited example showing a maximum kaolinite adsorption capacity near pH 5.5 (PDF p. 16). This is an example for a specific clay–antibiotic pair, not a universal optimum pH.

### Other antibiotic examples from REF-11

REF-11 reports pKa1/pKa2 values of 6.1/8.7 for ciprofloxacin, 9/12.5 for clarithromycin and 6.2/7.2 for trimethoprim (PDF p. 2). This supports explaining that different antibiotics have different speciation windows; do not reuse TC's pKa values for other compounds.

## 8. Other factors affecting adsorption

The selected sources identify several factors, but their effects are system-dependent. A factor may change antibiotic speciation, adsorbent surface properties, the number of available sites, the transport rate, or competition from other water constituents. The direction and magnitude of the response must be tied to the particular antibiotic, adsorbent and experimental conditions.

| Factor | What it can influence | Source-grounded qualification |
|---|---|---|
| pH | Antibiotic speciation and adsorbent surface charge; attraction or repulsion may change | The pKa values of TC, OTC and CIP differ. Kaolinite–OTC, NFO/BC–TC and activated-bagasse-biochar–CIP show different system-specific pH behaviour (REF-01, PDF pp. 2–3; REF-05, PDF p. 16; REF-13, PDF p. 7; REF-14, PDF pp. 7–8). |
| Ionic strength / competing ions | Can change the electrical double layer or competition for adsorption sites | Clay-mineral studies report a decrease in tetracycline adsorption as ionic strength increases (REF-05, PDF p. 16). In the NFO/BC–TC study, NaCl had no significant effect, whereas MgCl2 and CaCl2 reduced uptake (REF-13, PDF p. 8). Do not assume all salts have the same effect. |
| Natural organic matter (NOM) | May compete with antibiotic molecules for adsorbent sites | Increasing humic-acid dosage reduced TC removal by NFO/BC in the tested system (REF-13, PDF p. 8). |
| Adsorbent dose | Changes the amount of adsorbent and available sites relative to the pollutant | In the NFO/BC–TC study, removal rose with dose up to a point and then approached a plateau; more adsorbent also increased material cost (REF-13, PDF p. 7). |
| Initial antibiotic concentration | Changes the concentration gradient and the solute-to-site ratio | The selected experiments vary initial concentration, but the direction of change in removal percentage or capacity must be read from the specific data; do not assume both metrics move in the same direction (REF-09, PDF p. 2; REF-14, PDF p. 5; REF-15, PDF pp. 3–4). |
| Contact time | Determines how long transport and uptake can proceed before equilibrium | Equilibrium time differs substantially between adsorbent–antibiotic systems. For the bagasse-derived activated biochar and CIP, the authors report about 82% of the equilibrium uptake after 24 h and an equilibrium time of approximately 80 h under the stated conditions (REF-14, PDF pp. 1 and 5). |
| Temperature | May change equilibrium uptake and adsorption behaviour | CIP uptake by the activated bagasse biochar increased between 10 and 55 °C in that study; this is one system-specific result, not a universal temperature rule (REF-14, PDF pp. 1 and 8). |
| Surface area, pore structure and functional groups | Influence accessible sites and possible interactions | Surface area alone does not determine performance; pore accessibility, surface chemistry, speciation and solution matrix also matter (REF-02, PDF p. 9 / journal p. 4686; REF-04, PDF pp. 6–7; REF-12, PDF pp. 5–6). |
| Particle size | Is a preparation/operating variable that can differ among by-product sorbents | Rodríguez-López et al. report particle-size preparation details for hemp waste, oak ash and mussel shell, but this cited passage does not establish a universal directional effect of particle size on antibiotic uptake (REF-11, PDF p. 2). |

### Specific examples that must remain system-specific

**1. Tetracycline speciation (REF-01).** Gopal et al. report pKa values of approximately 3.3, 7.7 and 9.7 and show the pH-dependent species distribution in Figure 1 (PDF pp. 2–3; journal pp. 27082–27083). Their simplified text describes cationic TC below pH 3.3, zwitterionic TC between pH 3.3 and 7.7, and anionic TC above pH 7.7. The third pKa at approximately 9.7 marks further alkaline deprotonation; do not omit it from the diagram or imply that the species distribution changes at one sharp pH boundary.

**2. Oxytetracycline with kaolinite (REF-05).** The review reports OTC pKa values of 3.57, 7.49 and 9.88 and a maximum kaolinite adsorption capacity near pH 5.5 for the discussed system (PDF p. 16). This optimum must not be transferred to another clay, antibiotic or biochar.

**3. Tetracycline with NiFe2O4/biochar (REF-13).**
- The NFO/BC point of zero charge was approximately 5.35. In the tested system, TC removal was highest (92.4%) at pH 7.0; the authors explain the lower removal at pH 3 through electrostatic repulsion between positively charged NFO/BC and predominantly cationic TC (PDF p. 7).
- Raising NFO/BC dose from 0.2 to 1.0 g L−1 increased TC removal from 37% to 93.9%; further increase gave only a small additional removal benefit, while the adsorbent cost increased, so 1.0 g L−1 was selected for subsequent tests (PDF p. 7).
- Increasing humic-acid dosage significantly decreased TC removal (PDF p. 8).
- NaCl did not significantly affect TC adsorption in the reported tests, whereas MgCl2 and CaCl2 decreased adsorption capacity; at the reported 0.5% concentration, capacity fell to 59.8% and 63.8% of the original value, respectively (PDF p. 8).

**4. Ciprofloxacin with K2CO3-activated bagasse biochar (REF-14).**
- The authors report a pHPZC of approximately 8.1 for the activated biochar (PDF p. 7).
- At pH 6–10, they observed CPX precipitation. Their blank tests showed that the apparent removal at pH 7–9 was mainly precipitation rather than adsorption; these values must not be presented as adsorption performance (PDF p. 8).
- The authors therefore performed subsequent adsorption tests at pH 3.5 to minimise precipitation. At this pH CPX was predominantly cationic, while the activated-biochar surface was positively charged; the authors discuss hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions as possible contributions beyond electrostatic attraction (PDF pp. 7–8).
- The reported equilibrium uptake increased from 34.6 mg g−1 at 10 °C to 109.9 mg g−1 at 55 °C, showing endothermic behaviour for this particular system (PDF p. 8; abstract on PDF p. 1).
- About 82% of equilibrium uptake was reached after 24 h, while equilibrium was reached at approximately 80 h under the stated conditions (PDF pp. 1 and 5).

### Interpretation safeguards for Slide 8

1. pKa values belong to the antibiotic; pHPZC belongs to the adsorbent. They describe different properties and should be labelled separately.
2. pH effects arise from the combined speciation of the antibiotic and surface charge of the adsorbent. An optimum pH is specific to the tested material–antibiotic pair.
3. Apparent disappearance from solution is not automatically adsorption. Check whether precipitation, degradation or blank losses were controlled, particularly for ciprofloxacin at pH 6–10 in REF-14.
4. Do not claim that increasing dose, temperature, contact time or ionic strength always improves adsorption. Preserve salt-specific and matrix-specific differences.
5. Particle size is reported as a preparation parameter in REF-11; the cited passage does not establish a universal direction of its effect.

## 10. Claims that are not yet supported or should not be overstated

- A dedicated source-based formal definition of “aqueous medium” was not found; use the operational definition in Section 3.
- A dedicated adsorption-versus-absorption definition was not found in the inspected selected-paper passages; the distinction is standard teaching terminology and is labelled accordingly.
- Do not treat all antibiotics as having one common pKa/speciation profile.
- Do not state that high BET surface area alone determines adsorption capacity; surface chemistry, pore accessibility, speciation and matrix chemistry also matter in the selected papers.
- Do not say every biochar is automatically low-cost at the treatment-process level. Preparation, activation, separation, regeneration and disposal are separate cost components for the later economics section.
- Do not claim that one mechanism is universal or exclusive for all antibiotic–adsorbent pairs.
- Do not equate adsorption with degradation. The selected papers describe removal/retention through adsorption unless a coupled transformation is separately demonstrated.
