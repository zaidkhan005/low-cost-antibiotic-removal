## 1. What the topic is about

The presentation concerns the removal of antibiotic compounds from water-based systems. The selected papers discuss antibiotic residues in surface water, groundwater, rivers, lakes, wastewater-treatment effluents, hospital and pharmaceutical discharges, agricultural water, aquaculture and livestock-related wastewater. They describe antibiotics as contaminants of concern because residues can persist, affect aquatic organisms and microbial communities, and contribute to the selection or spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) and antibiotic-resistance genes (ARGs).

**Selected-source support**
- REF-02, pp. 4679–4680: antibiotic use, incomplete digestion/metabolism, detection in water and wastewater, hospital/household and pharmaceutical/industrial sources; structural classes and environmental distribution.
- REF-04, pp. 1–2: hospital effluents, industrial production waste, animal manure and irrigated agricultural land; detection in surface water, groundwater, seawater and soil; ARB/ARG context.
- REF-01, pp. 27082–27083: tetracycline structure/speciation, reported aqueous matrices, persistence and resistance context.
- REF-12, pp. 1–2: OTC/CIP in rivers, lakes, sewage effluent and livestock wastewater; weak biodegradability and persistence as described by the authors.
- REF-11, p. 2: CIP, clarithromycin and trimethoprim are selected as environmentally relevant antibiotic examples; the article discusses wastewater pathways and retention/release.

**Teaching qualification:** Avoid saying that every antibiotic is persistent in exactly the same way or that every conventional treatment plant has one universal removal percentage. The selected papers cover different compounds, matrices and treatment conditions. Present persistence and treatment limitations as concerns documented for the compounds and settings described by each source.

## 2. Antibiotics: definition and classification

For this presentation, introduce antibiotics as compounds used to prevent or treat bacterial infections. The papers emphasise their therapeutic use in humans and animals, alongside veterinary, livestock, aquaculture and other agricultural uses.

The selected sources use more than one classification scheme. REF-02 distinguishes bacteriostatic and bactericidal action and also lists structural/chemical classes. REF-05 lists major structure-based groups. REF-01 gives more detailed tetracycline subclasses and explains that tetracyclines differ by natural, semi-synthetic and synthetic preparation.

## 3. What is an aqueous medium?

**Operational teaching definition:** An aqueous medium is a liquid system in which water is the solvent or continuous liquid phase. In the selected studies it may be a prepared antibiotic solution, synthetic wastewater, or a real environmental/wastewater sample.

This is a plain-language chemistry definition for the presentation; the inspected papers use terms such as “aqueous solution”, “water”, “wastewater”, “surface water” and “aqueous matrix” but do not provide a standalone formal definition of “aqueous medium”.

Do not treat deionised laboratory water, synthetic wastewater and real wastewater as interchangeable. The matrix can contain dissolved ions, natural organic matter and other constituents that compete for adsorption sites or alter solution chemistry. REF-04 discusses natural-organic-matter competition in carbon adsorption; REF-02 identifies competing ions as a relevant factor; REF-11 examines three antibiotics in contact with different by-products and flow-chamber release conditions.

## 4. Adsorption terminology

The selected papers use the following terms throughout their experimental descriptions:

- **Adsorbate:** the dissolved species being captured; in this project, the antibiotic molecule or antibiotic species in the aqueous phase.
- **Adsorbent:** the solid material providing the surface, pores and/or active sites; examples in the collection include activated carbon, biochar, clays, magnetic composites and waste-derived by-products.
- **Adsorption:** accumulation or retention of molecules at an interface, particularly at the surface or accessible pore surfaces of a solid in contact with water.
- **Absorption:** transfer of a substance into the bulk of another phase, rather than its accumulation at an interface.
- **Sorption:** a broader term sometimes used when adsorption and absorption are not distinguished.
- **Biosorption:** REF-11 describes this in relation to sorbents derived from biological material, including microorganisms, plant biomass, agricultural residues and marine residues. It contrasts this material-origin use with adsorption, where the sorbent origin is not specified (REF-11, p. 2).

**Source boundary for the distinction:** REF-11 explicitly distinguishes adsorption and biosorption by the origin of the sorbent. The inspected selected-paper passages do not give a dedicated formal comparison of adsorption versus absorption. The adsorption/absorption distinction above is therefore a standard working chemistry explanation supplied for teaching, not a definition quoted or specifically established by one of the 15 papers. It should be presented as terminology, not as a finding from the review corpus.

### Adsorption sequence described in the biomass review

REF-02 describes the principal steps in the process for biomass-based adsorbents (PDF p. 9; journal p. 4686):

1. Movement of adsorbate molecules from the bulk matrix toward the adsorbent.
2. Diffusion through the liquid film surrounding the adsorbent particle.
3. Movement of adsorbate molecules toward accessible active sites.
4. Interaction between the adsorbate and adsorbent surface.

The review states that the adsorption mechanism depends on the final interaction step. This makes a useful original process diagram: **bulk water → liquid-film transport → surface/pore access → surface interaction/retention**.

**Wording caution:** The source text has a likely typographical phrase “adsorbent–adsorbent interactions” in this passage. The surrounding context is about adsorbate molecules reaching active sites; the slide should use the chemically meaningful phrasing “adsorbate–adsorbent interaction” and note this as an editorial clarification if quoting the source wording.

| Family | Description/examples from the selected sources | Main teaching point |

| Engineered composites | Biochar combined with metal oxides/ferrites, graphene oxide or other components | Modification can add functional groups, magnetic response or new interaction sites; it also adds preparation complexity (REF-13, pp. 1–2; REF-12, p. 1; REF-02, p. 4686) |

### Biochar and activated biochar: source-derived description

REF-02 describes biochar as a carbon-rich product of biomass pyrolysis in an inert atmosphere, with properties dependent on heating temperature, biomass type, carrier gas and catalysts (PDF p. 9; journal p. 4686). It notes that modification/activation may improve surface functionality and porosity, while bare biochars may show weaker affinity when surface functionality or porosity is insufficient.

REF-14 describes biochar as a carbon-rich material obtained by heating biomass at relatively low temperatures (<700 °C) in an oxygen-poor atmosphere; it lists surface area, porous structure, functional groups and mineral components as relevant properties (PDF p. 2). REF-14 also lists physical activation and chemical activation routes.

**Do not conflate terms:** Raw biochar, activated biochar and commercial activated carbon are related but not automatically identical. REF-04 explicitly discusses the terminology and describes activation as a key distinction. For the classification slide, keep “biochar” and “activated carbon/activated biochar” as separate labels.

## 6. Adsorption mechanisms discussed in the selected literature

These are proposed or reported contributing interactions; they are not universal mechanisms that occur in every adsorbent–antibiotic pair.

### 6.1 Electrostatic interactions

Attraction or repulsion can occur between charged antibiotic species and charged adsorbent surfaces. The sign and magnitude depend on the antibiotic's acid–base speciation and the surface charge under the solution conditions.

- REF-04 identifies electrostatic interaction as a primary mechanism for activated-carbon/antibiotic interactions and also discusses hydrophobic, π–π, electron-donor–acceptor and hydrogen-bond interactions (PDF p. 7).
- REF-05 explains the negative basal charge of clay minerals, pH-dependent edge charge and the role of solution pH in antibiotic speciation and adsorption (PDF pp. 5–7).
- REF-01 gives tetracycline pKa values of 3.3, 7.7 and 9.7 and describes cationic, zwitterionic and anionic forms over different pH regions (PDF p. 2).
- REF-12 reports electrostatic interactions as one contributor to OTC/CIP adsorption on its magnetic cotton-husk biochar (PDF p. 1).

### 6.2 Hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen bonding may involve suitable donor/acceptor groups on the antibiotic and functional groups on the adsorbent surface. The selected sources describe oxygen-containing and nitrogen-containing surface groups as relevant examples.

- REF-04 lists –COOH, –OH and –NH2 groups and identifies hydrogen bonding among the interactions discussed for carbon-based adsorbents (PDF p. 7).
- REF-13's graphical abstract (PDF p. 2) illustrates hydrogen bonding between tetracycline and the NiFe2O4/biochar composite.
- REF-12 reports hydroxyl, carbonyl and Fe–O groups in the magnetic cotton-husk biochar and identifies H-bonding among the proposed contributing interactions (PDF p. 1).

### 6.3 π–π interactions and π–π electron-donor–acceptor interactions

These interactions are discussed for aromatic antibiotic structures and carbonaceous surfaces that contain conjugated/aromatic domains.

- REF-04 lists π–π interactions and π–π electron-donor–acceptor interactions among antibiotic–carbon interactions (PDF p. 7).
- REF-02 lists π–π electron-donor–acceptor interactions in its biomass/biochar discussion (PDF p. 9; journal p. 4686).
- REF-13's graphical abstract (PDF p. 2) depicts π–π interaction for tetracycline on NiFe2O4/biochar.
- REF-12 reports π–π conjugation as a contributing interaction for OTC/CIP adsorption (PDF p. 1).

### 6.4 Hydrophobic interactions / hydrophobic partitioning

The selected reviews describe hydrophobic interaction as one possible route for antibiotic association with carbonaceous or biochar materials. REF-14 also lists hydrophobic partition among mechanisms reported for pharmaceutical adsorption. Its importance depends on both the pollutant and adsorbent chemistry; do not assume that every antibiotic behaves as hydrophobic.

**Source:** REF-04, PDF p. 7; REF-02, PDF p. 9; REF-14, PDF p. 2.

### 6.5 Pore filling

Pore filling refers to antibiotic molecules entering and occupying accessible pores in a porous adsorbent. Pore size distribution and accessibility therefore matter, not only the headline BET surface-area value.

- REF-04 discusses pore structure as important for carbon-based adsorbents and includes pore filling in its TC/biochar discussion (PDF pp. 6–7).
- REF-14 lists pore filling among reported pharmaceutical adsorption mechanisms (PDF p. 2).
- REF-13's graphical abstract (PDF p. 2) depicts pore filling.
- REF-02 describes the importance of porosity and pore structure in biochar/activated carbon (PDF p. 9; journal p. 4686).

### 6.6 Ion exchange, cation bridging and surface complexation

- REF-05 explains that clay-mineral layer charge is compensated by exchangeable cations, that these cations can exchange with solution cations, and that swelling clays may exchange cations on internal and external basal surfaces (PDF p. 6).
- REF-14 lists ion exchange, metal complexing and cation bridging among reported mechanisms for pharmaceutical pollutants (PDF p. 2).
- REF-12 reports Fe–O complexation for its iron-containing magnetic biochar (PDF p. 1).
- REF-13 depicts electrostatic force as well as hydrogen bonding, π–π interaction and pore filling (PDF p. 2).

**Mechanistic caution:** Do not infer a specific surface complex or chemical bond merely because a kinetic or isotherm model fits. The selected papers sometimes report mechanisms on the basis of multiple characterisation and solution-chemistry observations; the slide should say “proposed/contributing mechanism” unless direct evidence is supplied.

## 7. Why pH and antibiotic speciation are central

pH can affect two things at once:

1. The ionic form of the antibiotic (protonated, zwitterionic or deprotonated species).
2. The protonation state and surface charge of the adsorbent.

The result can be electrostatic attraction or repulsion, and may alter the overall adsorption behaviour.

### Tetracycline example from REF-01

REF-01 reports three pKa values: approximately 3.3, 7.7 and 9.7. It describes cationic TC below pH 3.3, zwitterionic TC between pH 3.3 and 7.7, and anionic TC above pH 7.7. The figure on PDF p. 3 (Figure 1) shows the TC structure and its pH-dependent species distribution (PDF pp. 2–3; journal pp. 27082–27083).

### Oxytetracycline–kaolinite example from REF-05

REF-05 reports pKa values of 3.57, 7.49 and 9.88 for oxytetracycline and describes four ionic fractions as pH changes. It discusses how the pH-dependent charge of kaolinite and OTC affects attraction/repulsion, with the cited example showing a maximum kaolinite adsorption capacity near pH 5.5 (PDF p. 16). This is an example for a specific clay–antibiotic pair, not a universal optimum pH.

### Other antibiotic examples from REF-11

REF-11 reports pKa1/pKa2 values of 6.1/8.7 for ciprofloxacin, 9/12.5 for clarithromycin and 6.2/7.2 for trimethoprim (PDF p. 2). This supports explaining that different antibiotics have different speciation windows; do not reuse TC's pKa values for other compounds.

## 10. Claims that are not yet supported or should not be overstated

- A dedicated source-based formal definition of “aqueous medium” was not found; use the operational definition in Section 3.
- A dedicated adsorption-versus-absorption definition was not found in the inspected selected-paper passages; the distinction is standard teaching terminology and is labelled accordingly.
- Do not treat all antibiotics as having one common pKa/speciation profile.
- Do not state that high BET surface area alone determines adsorption capacity; surface chemistry, pore accessibility, speciation and matrix chemistry also matter in the selected papers.
- Do not say every biochar is automatically low-cost at the treatment-process level. Preparation, activation, separation, regeneration and disposal are separate cost components for the later economics section.
- Do not claim that one mechanism is universal or exclusive for all antibiotic–adsorbent pairs.
- Do not equate adsorption with degradation. The selected papers describe removal/retention through adsorption unless a coupled transformation is separately demonstrated.
