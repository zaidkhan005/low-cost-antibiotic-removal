# Stage 2 Extraction 08 — Treatment Technologies for Antibiotic Removal

**Status:** Source-grounded extraction completed for Slide 4.  
**Source boundary:** The fixed 15-paper collection only; this note draws on REF-01, REF-04 and REF-15. No additional papers are introduced.  
**Purpose:** Compare the source-described roles and limitations of biological treatment, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), membrane filtration/reverse osmosis and adsorption. This is not a universal technology ranking.

---

## 1. Biological treatment

- **REF-04, PDF p. 2:** Teo et al. state that conventional biological wastewater treatments are unable to remove antibiotic residues efficiently and note high energy consumption. The article then lists several additional removal approaches, including adsorption, ultrasonic cavitation, AOPs, chlorination, electrochemical treatment and membrane processes.
- **REF-15, PDF p. 2:** Zhao et al. state that biological treatment processes are susceptible to environmental factors and may lead to the growth of antibiotic-resistant bacteria.
- **REF-01, PDF p. 4 / journal p. 27084:** Gopal et al. state that primary and secondary treatment of residual antibiotic wastewater was insufficient to remove 100% of tetracycline (TC) in the reviewed treatment context.

**Qualification:** These are review-level and source-specific statements. The TC statement from REF-01 is specific to tetracycline; do not turn it into a universal percentage or claim that every biological treatment system performs identically.

## 2. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs)

- **REF-01, PDF p. 4 / journal p. 27084:** Gopal et al. list photolysis, ozonation, Fenton and photo-Fenton processes and describe oxidation in the presence of ozone, UV and/or hydrogen peroxide as involving transformation and oxidised products.
- **REF-01, PDF p. 6 / journal p. 27086:** The review describes reactive radicals generated in oxidation systems and gives examples of TC degradation processes.
- **REF-01, PDF p. 11 / journal p. 27091:** The review warns that AOPs can release oxidised by-products that may be toxic to the environment.
- **REF-04, PDF p. 6:** Teo et al. describe advanced oxidation as an alternative oxidation approach involving highly reactive hydroxyl radicals and discuss ozonation, Fenton oxidation, photocatalysis and electrochemical processes. The review notes that AOP efficacy may be high but cost can be a disadvantage.
- **REF-15, PDF p. 2:** Zhao et al. state that oxidised products may have higher toxicity and cause secondary pollution.

**Qualification:** Do not equate disappearance of the parent antibiotic with complete mineralisation or detoxification. The selected sources specifically warrant attention to oxidised by-products and possible secondary toxicity.

## 3. Membrane filtration and reverse osmosis

- **REF-01, PDF p. 4 / journal p. 27084:** Gopal et al. list membrane filtration and reverse osmosis among advanced treatment techniques and compare their production/operational costs with adsorption and AOPs in the review's TC-treatment context.
- **REF-01, PDF p. 11 / journal p. 27091:** The review states that nanofiltration and ultrafiltration can remove antibiotics more effectively, but membrane fouling, high energy requirements and high operational cost limit their application. It also identifies high operational cost, fouling and ineffectiveness against residual antibiotic concentrations as disadvantages of reverse osmosis.

**Qualification:** Preserve the distinction between nanofiltration/ultrafiltration and reverse osmosis. Do not state that all membrane processes have the same limitations or assign them a universal cost ranking.

## 4. Adsorption

- **REF-01, PDF p. 4 / journal p. 27084:** Gopal et al. identify adsorption as a treatment approach used for TC and discuss its role alongside AOPs in tertiary treatment.
- **REF-01, PDF p. 6 / journal p. 27086:** The review defines adsorption as accumulation of matter from a gas or liquid at an adsorbent surface and describes it as simple, easy to operate and efficient in the context discussed.
- **REF-01, PDF p. 11 / journal p. 27091:** The review states that adsorption requires sorbent material that must be regenerated and properly disposed of after use; adsorbed antibiotic should be destroyed or appropriately recovered.
- **REF-04, PDF p. 6:** Teo et al. describe adsorption as mass transfer of chemical substances from the liquid phase to the solid phase. They note that physical treatment often does not involve pollutant breakdown; the pollutant is separated or concentrated instead.
- **REF-04, PDF p. 14:** Teo et al. identify high production and regeneration costs, disposal of secondary waste including spent adsorbent and recovered antibiotics, and possible secondary pollution from metal-modified carbon adsorbents as limitations.

**Qualification:** Adsorption transfers antibiotic from water to a solid phase; it does not, by itself, establish destruction or mineralisation. It is not accurate to present adsorption as a process with no waste-management burden merely because no oxidation by-products are formed.

## 5. Cross-technology interpretation for the presentation

| Technology | Source-supported role | Limitation or qualification to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Biological treatment | Microorganisms treat wastewater; conventional biological treatment can leave antibiotic residues | Removal may be inefficient; the selected source statements are not universal performance data |
| AOPs | Reactive oxidation chemistry transforms/degrades antibiotics | Oxidised by-products may be toxic; parent-compound removal must not be equated automatically with mineralisation or detoxification |
| Membrane filtration / reverse osmosis | Separates contaminants from the treated water stream | Fouling, energy demand and operating cost can limit use; the review gives additional limitations for reverse osmosis |
| Adsorption | Transfers dissolved antibiotic from the liquid phase to a solid adsorbent | Used adsorbent and captured antibiotic require recovery, regeneration and/or appropriate disposal |

**Teaching synthesis:** The table is a comparison of process roles and selected limitations, not a performance ranking. The sources do not support a single universally best technology across antibiotics, wastewater matrices and installations.
