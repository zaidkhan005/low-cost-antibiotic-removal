## 1. What “low-cost” must mean in an adsorption process

A low-cost feedstock does not automatically produce a low-cost treatment process. The adsorbent must be collected or purchased, prepared, converted, activated or modified, washed, separated from the treated water, regenerated or replaced, and eventually managed as spent material. The treatment process may also require pH adjustment, mixing, pumping, filtration or centrifugation, drying and management of regenerant liquids.

REF-03 identifies adsorbent consumption and regeneration as major cost determinants and explains that equipment, electricity, reactivation, transport and spent-adsorbent disposal also need to be considered when assessing scale-up (PDF pp. 19–20). It explicitly warns that a low-cost adsorbent that is difficult to regenerate may not be economically attractive (PDF p. 20).

REF-08 similarly frames cost-effectiveness as a combination of adsorbent production, process performance, regeneration, eluent and wastewater treatment, rather than a property of the raw feedstock alone (PDF pp. 7–8 and 14–15). The review is about pharmaceuticals and waste-derived adsorbents broadly, not exclusively antibiotics.

## 2. Cost components to account for

A defensible economic assessment should identify the following components separately.

| Cost block | Items to include | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Feedstock acquisition | Collection or purchase, transport, sorting, storage, competing uses | Some residues are not free at the point of use; wet or bulky feedstocks may cost more to handle |
| Feedstock preparation | Washing, dewatering, drying, crushing, grinding and sieving | Preparation can consume water, heat, labour and electricity |
| Adsorbent production | Pyrolysis/carbonisation, furnace time, inert gas, hydrothermal synthesis, milling | High temperature, long residence time and specialised equipment can dominate production energy |
| Chemical activation/modification | KOH, K\(_2\)CO\(_3\), acids, salts, ammonia and other reagents | Reagent purchase, neutralisation and rinse-water demand add cost |
| Adsorption operation | Adsorbent dose, contact time, pH adjustment, mixing, pumping, tanks and filtration | A high dose or long contact time raises equipment and operating requirements |
| Separation/recovery | Filtration, centrifugation, magnetic collection, immobilisation and adsorbent loss | A fine or non-magnetic adsorbent may be harder to recover; unrecovered solids need replacement |
| Regeneration/reuse | Eluent, ultrasound, heating, washing, drying, solvent recovery and capacity loss | Regeneration can reduce fresh adsorbent demand but creates additional utility and liquid-waste costs |
| Residual management | Spent adsorbent, antibiotic-loaded regenerant, wash streams, leached metals or organic compounds | The pollutant is transferred to another phase; secondary streams still require management |
| Capital and scale-up | Contactors, pumps, pipes, monitoring, corrosion control, maintenance and labour | Laboratory batch performance is not the same as a viable continuous treatment plant |

The table above is a costing framework synthesised from REF-03 (PDF pp. 19–20), REF-08 (PDF pp. 7–8 and 14–15), and the material-preparation and regeneration procedures in REF-12–REF-15. It is not a reported cost model from one of the antibiotic experiments.

## 3. Cost denominators: do not compare unlike quantities

A paper may report:
- cost per kilogram of adsorbent (€/kg);
- cost per litre or cubic metre of treated wastewater (€/L or €/m³);
- cost per mass of pollutant removed (e.g., €/g antibiotic removed);
- or only the price/cost of one reagent or preparation step.

These values are not interchangeable. A cost per kilogram of adsorbent does not tell the reader how much material is required to treat a given volume, and it does not include the cost of regeneration unless the paper says it does.

A simple material-cost contribution can be estimated from:

\[
\text{Adsorbent cost per treated volume}
=
\text{adsorbent price per kg}
\times
\text{adsorbent dose in kg per treated volume}
\]

This is only the fresh-adsorbent component. A full comparison must add preparation, activation, operation, recovery, regeneration, treatment of secondary liquids, and spent-adsorbent management. A useful reporting denominator is total cost per cubic metre treated or cost per gram of antibiotic removed, with the treatment conditions stated.

REF-03 specifically notes that required adsorbent concentration depends on initial pollutant concentration, removal ability and discharge limits (PDF p. 20). Therefore, an adsorption capacity quoted in mg g\(^{-1}\) cannot be converted into process cost without dose, influent concentration, target effluent concentration, working capacity, recovery and regeneration data.

## 4. What the selected antibiotic studies actually tell us about production and operation

### 4.1 REF-14 — K\(_2\)CO\(_3\)-activated barley-bagasse biochar for ciprofloxacin

**Feedstock context.** REF-14 identifies spent barley grains (brewing bagasse) as a by-product of beer production. It reports that producing 1000 L of beer generates about 200 kg of wet spent grains, with 70–80% water content. The paper says that spent grains are commonly used as animal feed because of their protein and fibre content (PDF p. 2).

The feedstock is therefore a by-product with potential value and an existing use; it should not automatically be treated as free or available without competing demand. Its high water content also means drying is part of the preparation route.

**Preparation steps reported by REF-14 (PDF pp. 3–4):**
1. Dry bagasse at 60–70 °C, crush and sieve.
2. Pyrolyse the 1.0–2.5 mm fraction under argon, heating to 400 °C and holding for 2 h.
3. Crush and sieve the biochar again.
4. Suspend 5.0 g of biochar in 100 mL of 2 mol L\(^{-1}\) K\(_2\)CO\(_3\), with stirring for 24 h.
5. Centrifuge and dry the solid at 105 °C.
6. Heat the activated material at 800 °C for 3 h.
7. Wash repeatedly with distilled water until stable pH (approximately 7) and dry at 60–70 °C.

REF-14 explains that K\(_2\)CO\(_3\) is less corrosive, easier to store and more environmentally friendly than KOH (PDF p. 2). That is a comparative rationale stated by the authors; the paper does not provide a reagent-cost comparison or a complete environmental life-cycle assessment.

**Practical performance and gaps.** The paper tests CPX in batch flasks and reports a maximum capacity around 105 mg g\(^{-1}\) under pH 3.5 and 25 °C. Its conclusion calls for further work with real effluents and regeneration/reuse (REF-14, PDF pp. 1 and 13). It does not provide a full production-cost estimate, energy inventory, adsorbent yield, cost per cubic metre treated or completed regeneration-cost analysis.

### 4.2 REF-15 — One-step porous biochar from oxytetracycline fermentation residues

**Feedstock context.** REF-15 describes antibiotic fermentation residues as a large-volume waste from antibiotic production. It reports approximately 10 tonnes of residue per tonne of antibiotic produced and a high moisture content of 79–92% (PDF p. 2). The authors identify these residues as hazardous waste and describe their reuse as a biochar precursor as a waste-management opportunity.

**Preparation steps reported by REF-15 (PDF pp. 2–3):**
1. Dry oxytetracycline fermentation residues at 105 °C to constant mass.
2. Grind and sieve the material.
3. Mix with KOH at selected mass ratios (1:1, 1:2 or 1:3).
4. Pyrolyse under nitrogen at 500, 600 or 700 °C, with a 2 °C min\(^{-1}\) heating rate and 2 h holding time.
5. Wash with 0.1 mol L\(^{-1}\) HCl until non-alkaline, then rinse with deionized water to pH 7.
6. Vacuum-dry at 80 °C for 12 h.

The authors describe the method as a one-step controllable pyrolysis route without a separate preliminary carbonisation step and state that reuse of the waste reduces production costs (REF-15, PDF pp. 1 and 16).

**Cost-evidence limitation.** The preparation is presented as time- and cost-saving, but the article does not provide a full cost table, adsorbent yield, KOH/HCl consumption per kilogram of product, furnace energy per kilogram, nitrogen consumption, water use, or cost per volume of antibiotic wastewater treated. The source supports a qualitative cost-saving claim, not a quantified production or operating cost.

The high feedstock moisture also means drying is a necessary step; any energy or cost saving must be evaluated against this and the subsequent chemical activation, acid washing, water rinsing and vacuum drying.

### 4.3 REF-12 — Magnetic ball-milled cotton-husk biochar for OTC and CIP

REF-12 reports that cotton husks were purchased from a resource-recycling supplier, crushed to about 0.15 mm and pyrolysed at 800 °C for 1 h under nitrogen (PDF pp. 3–4). The Fe\(_2\)O\(_3\)-supported composite route uses a cotton-husk-to-\(\gamma\)-Fe\(_2\)O\(_3\) mass ratio of 20:1, ball milling at 350 rpm for 2.5 h, and further pyrolysis under the same conditions (PDF p. 3).

The magnetic property is intended to simplify adsorbent separation after treatment (REF-12, PDF pp. 4 and 10). However, the study does not quantify magnetic recovery percentage, mass loss over cycles, cost of the iron precursor, nitrogen use, furnace energy, ball-milling energy or total cost per treated volume.

Its ethanol–ultrasound regeneration procedure reports 0.5 L ethanol and 0.5 L rinse water for 50 mg adsorbent, with 500 W ultrasound for 60 min (REF-12, PDF pp. 3–4). This provides process conditions that matter to scale-up; it is not an economic cost calculation. The solvent volume and energy demand should be assessed before treating the regeneration route as inexpensive at larger scale.

### 4.4 REF-13 — NiFe\(_2\)O\(_4\)/biochar composite from poplar sawdust

REF-13 describes poplar sawdust collected from a local wood-processing plant, cleaned, dried and carbonised at 700 °C for 1 h to produce biochar (PDF p. 3). The composite is then made by dispersing biochar in water, sonicating, adding nickel and iron nitrate salts, adjusting pH with concentrated ammonia, and carrying out hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C for 12 h. The product is centrifuged, washed repeatedly with deionized water and ethanol, and vacuum-dried at 80 °C for 12 h (PDF pp. 3–4).

The material's magnetism is reported as an advantage for separation (REF-13, PDF p. 8). Yet the source does not provide a quantified cost per kilogram, production yield, reagent/energy inventory, magnetic recovery efficiency, or cost per treated volume. The use of metal salts, ammonia, sonication, hydrothermal synthesis, centrifugation, repeated solvent washing and vacuum drying should be included in any future cost analysis.

### 4.5 REF-11 — Hemp waste, oak ash and mussel shell by-products

REF-11 compares three by-products as adsorbents for ciprofloxacin, clarithromycin and trimethoprim using batch and stirred-flow chamber experiments (PDF pp. 1–4). Its focus is antibiotic retention, release and pH-dependent behaviour, not a full economic assessment.

The study gives a useful practical warning: antibiotics retained by a by-product may be released again under flow or changing conditions (REF-11, PDF pp. 3–6). Therefore, a material's initial sorption capacity is not enough to establish reliable treatment. The paper does not report feedstock collection/transport costs, adsorbent preparation cost, continuous plant operating cost, regeneration cost or treatment cost per volume.

## 5. Cost examples from REF-08 — useful framework, not antibiotic cost estimates

REF-08 reviews pharmaceutical adsorption and waste-derived adsorbent economics. It contains cost examples largely for ibuprofen, BPA, diclofenac and other non-antibiotic pollutants. These examples can illustrate cost-accounting methods but must not be presented as costs for ciprofloxacin, tetracycline or oxytetracycline.

**Examples from REF-08 Table 5 (PDF p. 13):**

| Adsorbent example | Reported cost | Cost basis/context |
|---|---:|---|
| Raw date-stone biochar (DSPB) | 1.67 €/kg | IBU; preparation cost reported in review |
| Chemically activated date-stone carbon (DSCC) | 1.49 €/kg | IBU; preparation cost reported in review |
| Raw biochar (TRB) | 2.67 €/kg | IBU; includes collection, size reduction, drying, carbonisation and overhead |
| Chemically activated carbon (TCAB) | 2.99 €/kg | IBU; includes preparation and activation-related costs |
| Plane-tree leaf biochar (P-BC) | 3.70 €/kg | IBU; drying, carbonisation and other costs |
| Pine-cone activated carbon (PC-H / PC-Z) | 75.04 / 68.64 €/kg | BPA; includes raw-material preparation, carbonisation, activation and neutralisation |

These are review-compiled costs for particular materials and pollutants. They are not directly comparable with one another unless their process boundaries, yields, reagent costs, year, scale and overhead assumptions match.

REF-08 also reports P-BC treatment cost of 0.004 €/L of wastewater for an ibuprofen case and shell biochar at 0.009 €/L, with the latter figure explicitly excluding regeneration expenses (PDF p. 14). These are non-antibiotic examples and should not be used as a cost estimate for antibiotic removal.

**Source consistency caution:** REF-08's Table 5 lists Melia azedarach fruit biochar (MFC) at 1,062.50 €/kg (PDF p. 13), while its conclusion describes the cost range of waste-derived adsorbents as 1.49–75.04 €/kg (PDF p. 15). The supplied text does not reconcile this value. Do not silently omit or harmonise the discrepancy if quoting the range; safest is to state selected examples and flag that the review contains a table-versus-summary inconsistency.

REF-08 reports that regeneration is estimated to account for approximately 60% of adsorption-system costs, citing another study (PDF p. 14). This is a review-reported estimate, not a universal constant and not a quantified figure for any of the selected antibiotic adsorbents.

## 6. Scale-up and environmental checks

### 6.1 Laboratory batch performance is not a plant cost

REF-03 notes that many published adsorption studies remain at laboratory scale, with limited pilot studies, commercialisation and detailed economic analyses (PDF p. 19). Scale-up also requires appropriate adsorbent dose, discharge limits, hydraulic conditions, contact time, and equipment.

### 6.2 Recovery and mass loss

Magnetic separation (REF-12 and REF-13) can simplify collection, but a practical study still needs to measure:
- fraction of adsorbent recovered;
- particle escape into treated water;
- adsorbent mass lost in washing and regeneration;
- metal or organic leaching;
- recovered adsorbent capacity over repeated cycles.

A sorbent that is cheap to make but difficult to recover may require frequent replacement and can become a secondary contaminant.

### 6.3 Regeneration and secondary streams

REF-03 and REF-08 both identify regeneration and spent-adsorbent disposal as parts of the total treatment cost (REF-03, PDF pp. 19–20; REF-08, PDF pp. 7–8 and 14). Regeneration transfers antibiotic from the solid to an eluent, creating a secondary liquid that needs treatment or controlled recovery. Solvent recovery, neutralisation, washing, drying and leaching control can materially affect the economics.

### 6.4 Real-water validation

The selected primary studies mainly use model aqueous solutions or simulated wastewater conditions. REF-14 calls for additional work with real effluents and regeneration/reuse (PDF p. 13). REF-11 includes stirred-flow release experiments, but it is not a full treatment-plant pilot or cost assessment. A cost estimate should use representative wastewater composition, competing constituents, target discharge limits and continuous operation.

## 7. Evidence gaps across the selected corpus

The fixed-source collection provides strong laboratory evidence for adsorption, but the following data are generally missing or not comparable for the antibiotic adsorbents in the primary studies:

- production yield (kg adsorbent per kg wet/dry feedstock);
- full reagent, water and inert-gas consumption per kg product;
- energy per kg product and per regeneration cycle;
- feedstock transport, storage and seasonal availability;
- adsorbent recovery percentage and mass loss;
- cost of eluent, washing, neutralisation and spent-regenerant treatment;
- real-wastewater and continuous-flow performance at the required discharge limit;
- full cost per m³ treated or per gram of antibiotic removed;
- comparable life-cycle or techno-economic assessments across the antibiotic adsorbents.

A claim such as “waste-derived”, “green” or “cost-saving” should therefore be kept separate from a demonstrated cost per treatment volume.

## 8. Slide 14 content recommendation

**Title:** Low-Cost Potential — Is the Whole Treatment Process Affordable?

Recommended sequence:

1. **Start with the cost chain:** feedstock → preparation → activation/modification → adsorption operation → separation/recovery → regeneration → residual-waste treatment.
2. **Show what the selected antibiotic papers actually demonstrate:** useful feedstocks and laboratory adsorption; some magnetic recovery and multi-cycle reuse; no comparable full cost per m³ for the antibiotic systems.
3. **Use REF-08 as an illustrative cost-accounting example:** cost per kg and cost per litre are different denominators, and its cited cost examples are mainly non-antibiotic pollutants.
4. **Use REF-14 and REF-15 to expose hidden processing costs:** high-moisture feedstocks, drying, high-temperature treatment, chemical activation, washing and drying.
5. **Use REF-12/REF-13 to show recovery trade-offs:** magnetic separation can help, but synthesis, metal salts, milling, hydrothermal processing, ethanol, ultrasound and rinse water must be counted.
6. **Close with a practical verdict:** “Waste-derived feedstock can reduce raw-material cost, but low-cost treatment is only demonstrated when preparation, operation, recovery, regeneration and disposal are all accounted for.”

Keep the distinction visible: laboratory adsorption capacity is a technical performance metric; it is not a cost-effectiveness result.
