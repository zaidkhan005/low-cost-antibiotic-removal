## Slide 3 — Why Are Antibiotic Residues Concerning?

1. **Environmental occurrence:** reported in surface water, groundwater, seawater, soil and wastewater-related matrices.
2. **Ecological effects:** residues may affect aquatic organisms and microbial communities.
3. **Antimicrobial resistance:** environmental exposure raises concern about antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) and antibiotic-resistance genes (ARGs).
4. **Treatment challenge:** conventional biological treatment may remove antibiotic residues inefficiently.

**Visual:** Four simple blocks or a left-to-right chain:
Environmental occurrence → Ecological effects → AMR concern → Treatment challenge.

**Citation on slide:** (Gopal et al., 2020; Teo et al., 2022; Ajala et al., 2023)

**Presenter emphasis:** Do not imply adsorption of dissolved antibiotic molecules directly removes ARB or ARGs. Gopal et al.'s primary/secondary treatment limitation is specifically discussed for tetracycline; do not generalise it to every antibiotic or treatment plant.

## Slide 4 — Technologies for Antibiotic Removal

| Technology | Role |
|---|---|
| Biological treatment | Microorganisms treat wastewater |
| Advanced oxidation | Reactive oxidation transforms/degrades antibiotics |
| Membrane filtration / reverse osmosis | Separates contaminants from the water stream |
| Adsorption | Transfers antibiotics from water to a solid |

**Key statement:** Adsorption transfers antibiotic from the liquid phase to a solid phase. It does not, by itself, prove degradation or mineralisation.

**Visual:** The simple two-column table shown in the user's slide example. Optional lower strip: antibiotic-containing water → adsorbent contact → treated liquid + loaded adsorbent.

**Citation on slide:** (Gopal et al., 2020; Teo et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2024)

**Presenter emphasis:** Biological treatment, AOPs and membranes have their own limitations. AOP transformation is not automatically complete mineralisation; oxidised by-products may remain. Adsorption creates a loaded solid that must be recovered, regenerated or disposed of appropriately.

## Slide 5 — Fundamentals of Adsorption

**Adsorbate:** dissolved antibiotic species being captured.  
**Adsorbent:** solid material providing surface, pores and/or active sites.  
**Adsorption:** accumulation or retention at an interface.  
**Absorption:** uptake into the bulk of another phase.

**Principal steps:**
1. Movement from bulk water toward the adsorbent
2. Diffusion through the surrounding liquid film
3. Movement toward accessible active sites
4. Final adsorbate–adsorbent interaction

**Figure to use:** Ajala et al. (2023), Fig. 3 — detailed scheme of sorption mechanisms on a biomass-based material. Use as a supporting illustration, not as a replacement for the four-step transport sequence.

**Caption under image:** *Figure 2. Representative sorption mechanisms on a biomass-based material. Reproduced from Ajala et al. (2023).*

**Citation on slide:** (Ajala et al., 2023; Rodríguez-López et al., 2024)

**Presenter emphasis:** Pore filling is a possible adsorption/retention mode, not a compulsory fifth transport step. The adsorption/absorption definition is standard chemistry background; identify it as such rather than attributing it to a particular paper.

## Slide 7 — Mechanisms of Interaction Between Antibiotics and Adsorbents

**This is a separate, dedicated mechanisms slide.**

**CARBON-BASED ADSORBENTS**
- Electrostatic interactions
- Hydrogen bonding
- π–π / electron-donor–acceptor interactions
- Hydrophobic interactions
- Possible pore filling

**NATURAL CLAY MINERALS**
- Basal and edge electrostatic interactions
- pH-dependent edge charge
- Exchangeable-cation interactions / cation exchange
- Interlayer uptake in some swelling clays

**IRON-CONTAINING BIOCHAR COMPOSITES**
- Electrostatic interactions
- Hydrogen bonding
- π–π interactions
- Fe–O complexation (reported/proposed for Fe₂O₃@BMBC)
- Pore filling shown in the NFO/BC–TC conceptual model

**Figures to use:**
- Teo et al. (2022), Fig. 6 — carbon-based adsorbent–antibiotic interactions.
- Perelomov et al. (2025), Fig. 4 — proposed adsorption mechanisms by natural and modified clays.
- Chen et al. (2024), Fig. 6 — proposed OTC removal mechanism by Fe₂O₃@BMBC.
- Liang et al. (2024), graphical abstract — authors' conceptual TC/NiFe₂O₄–biochar mechanism.

**Recommended layout:** Three panels (carbon / clay / iron-containing composite), each with one relevant figure and a short list. Do not place all mechanisms on one generic particle.

**Citation on slide:** (Teo et al., 2022; Perelomov et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024)

**Scientific qualification:** These are material-specific reported or proposed interactions, not a universal checklist. Meseguer et al. (2024) give a broad literature list of possible mechanisms; this does not establish all of them for their own CPX–biochar system. A kinetic or isotherm fit alone does not prove a molecular mechanism.

## Slide 8 — Factors Affecting Adsorption

**Main factors:**
1. **pH:** controls antibiotic speciation and adsorbent surface charge.
2. **Adsorbent properties:** surface area, pore structure, functional groups, surface charge and particle size.
3. **Adsorbent dose:** changes the number of available sites relative to the amount of antibiotic.
4. **Initial concentration:** changes the solute-to-site ratio and the equilibrium uptake.
5. **Contact time:** controls how close the system gets to equilibrium.
6. **Temperature:** may change equilibrium uptake; the direction is system-dependent.
7. **Ionic strength / competing ions:** salts may affect charge interactions or compete at surfaces.
8. **Natural organic matter:** may compete with antibiotics for sites.

**Important distinction:** Antibiotic pKa and adsorbent point of zero charge (pHₚzc) are different properties.

**Visual:** A central adsorbent particle surrounded by eight labelled factor arrows. Add a small pH scale/speciation bar for tetracycline with pKa markers 3.3, 7.7 and 9.7.

**Citation on slide:** (Gopal et al., 2020; Perelomov et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2024; Meseguer et al., 2024)

**Presenter emphasis:** Effects are system-specific. Do not claim increasing dose, temperature, time or ionic strength always improves adsorption. State the antibiotic, material and conditions for each directional result.

## Slide 9 — Experimental Evidence: Factors Affecting Adsorption

**Liang et al. (2024): NiFe₂O₄/biochar + tetracycline**
- Increasing dose from 0.2 to 1.0 g L⁻¹ raised removal from 37% to 93.9%; further dose increases gave little added benefit.
- Humic acid reduced TC removal.
- NaCl had no significant effect in the reported tests; MgCl₂ and CaCl₂ reduced capacity.

**Meseguer et al. (2024): activated bagasse biochar + ciprofloxacin**
- Blank tests showed apparent CPX disappearance at pH 7–9 was mainly precipitation, not adsorption.
- pH 3.5 was used in subsequent experiments to minimise precipitation.
- Increasing initial concentration raised qₑ while percentage removal decreased.
- Equilibrium uptake increased from 34.6 mg g⁻¹ at 10 °C to 109.9 mg g⁻¹ at 55 °C.
- About 82% of equilibrium uptake was reached after 24 h; equilibrium at approximately 80 h.

**Figures to use:**
- Liang et al. (2024), Fig. 4 — adsorption performance, dose, zeta potential, pH, humic acid, ionic strength and reuse.
- Meseguer et al. (2024), Fig. 3 — pH effect with and without adsorbent.
- Optional small inset: Meseguer et al. (2024), Fig. 6 — initial concentration.

**Caption:** *Experimental effects of operating and solution conditions on antibiotic adsorption. Source: Liang et al. (2024), Fig. 4; Meseguer et al. (2024), Figs. 3 and 6.*

**Citation on slide:** (Liang et al., 2024; Meseguer et al., 2024)

**Scientific qualification:** Keep the studies separate; these are not head-to-head comparisons. CPX disappearance at pH 7–9 in Meseguer et al. is mainly precipitation, not adsorption.

**Presenter emphasis:** Capacity and removal percentage answer different questions. Label the reconstructed (C_e) and (Y) as derived, not directly reported values. Do not use the sign-anomalous positive-uptake equation printed by Zhao et al.; use the consistent positive mass-balance form above.

## Slide 11 — Adsorption Isotherms

**An adsorption isotherm** describes the equilibrium relationship between adsorbed amount ((q_e)) and equilibrium solution concentration ((C_e)) at a stated temperature.

| Model | Main idea |
|---|---|
| Langmuir | Idealised finite monolayer capacity |
| Freundlich | Empirical heterogeneous surface; no explicit finite saturation capacity |
| Temkin | Adsorption heat changes with surface coverage |
| Sips | Hybrid model combining heterogeneity with finite saturation |

**Figures to use:**
- Liang et al. (2024), Fig. 5 — TC isotherms on BC, NFO and NFO/BC.
- Meseguer et al. (2024), Fig. 7 — CPX isotherm fits (Langmuir, Freundlich, Sips, Redlich–Peterson and Toth).

**Citation on slide:** (Ayawei et al., 2017; Mirizadeh et al., 2023; Egbedina et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2024; Meseguer et al., 2024)

**Presenter emphasis:** An isotherm is not a kinetic curve. Fitted capacities are model parameters, not necessarily directly measured plateaus. Do not select a model from R² alone; consider residuals and error criteria.

## Slide 12 — Adsorption Kinetics

**Kinetics** describes how adsorption uptake changes with time.

- (q_t): amount adsorbed at time (t)
- (q_e): amount adsorbed at equilibrium

**Common models:**
- Pseudo-first-order (PFO)
- Pseudo-second-order (PSO)
- Intraparticle diffusion (Weber–Morris)
- Elovich model

**Figures to use:**
- Liang et al. (2024), Fig. 6 — TC adsorption kinetics.
- Meseguer et al. (2024), Figs. 4 and 5 — CPX kinetic-model fits and intraparticle-diffusion plot.
- Chen et al. (2024), Fig. 4 — OTC/CIP PFO/PSO and intraparticle-diffusion plots.

**Citation on slide:** (Vareda, 2023; Liang et al., 2024; Meseguer et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024)

**Presenter emphasis:** PFO or PSO model preference does not prove physisorption, chemisorption or one unique rate-limiting mechanism. Compare conditions before comparing reported contact times.

## Slide 15 — Low-Cost Potential and Practical Feasibility

**Low-cost evaluation must include the full process:**
- Feedstock acquisition and preparation
- Drying, pyrolysis and activation
- Chemicals, washing and water
- Energy and equipment
- Adsorbent dose and recovery
- Regeneration and reuse
- Eluent treatment and secondary-waste disposal

**Fresh adsorbent contribution (per volume treated):**

[
C_{mathrm{adsorbent},V}=P_{mathrm{ads}}	imes D_{mathrm{ads}}
]

where (P_{mathrm{ads}}) is adsorbent price (currency kg⁻¹) and (D_{mathrm{ads}}) is dose (kg m⁻³).

**Key point:** Cost per kg of adsorbent is not the same as cost per m³ of water treated.

**Citation on slide:** (Kyzas & Kostoglou, 2014; Gkika et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024; Meseguer et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024)

**Presenter emphasis:** The selected antibiotic studies do not provide comparable complete production or treatment-cost calculations. Gkika et al. (2025) is pharmaceutical-wide context, not exclusively antibiotic evidence. Waste-derived does not automatically mean zero-cost or low-cost.

## Slide 16 — Conclusion

1. **Performance is system-specific:** antibiotic speciation, adsorbent surface properties and water matrix matter.
2. **Adsorption is separation:** it transfers antibiotics to a solid; it does not prove degradation.
3. **Metrics are not interchangeable:** capacity, removal, fitted capacity, model fit and capacity retention measure different things.
4. **Reuse needs a full cycle:** regeneration, recovery and antibiotic-containing secondary streams matter.
5. **Waste-derived does not automatically mean low-cost:** preparation, dose, energy, recovery and disposal must be included.

**Take-home statement:** Relevant-water performance + recoverability + whole-process economics.
