## Slide 2 — ANTIBIOTICS AND THEIR SOURCES

**Antibiotics** are compounds used to prevent or treat bacterial infections in humans and animals. (Ajala et al., 2023; Teo et al., 2022)

**Major pathways into water**
1. Human and hospital use → household and hospital wastewater. (Ajala et al., 2023; Teo et al., 2022)
2. Pharmaceutical production → industrial discharges and wastewater. (Teo et al., 2022)
3. Livestock, aquaculture and agriculture → manure, runoff and agricultural drainage. (Ajala et al., 2023; Teo et al., 2022)
4. Wastewater effluents and contaminated soil can connect these pathways to surface water and groundwater. (Ajala et al., 2023)

**Examples of antibiotic groups**
- Tetracyclines — tetracycline (TC), oxytetracycline (OTC). (Gopal et al., 2020; Teo et al., 2022)
- Fluoroquinolones — ciprofloxacin (CIP). (Teo et al., 2022; Meseguer et al., 2024)
- Sulfonamides — sulfamethazine and sulfamethoxazole. (Teo et al., 2022)
- Macrolides — clarithromycin. (Rodríguez-López et al., 2024)
- β-Lactams — amoxicillin and related compounds. (Teo et al., 2022)

**Suggested figure:** Ajala et al. (2023), Fig. 1 — *Common sources and distributions of antibiotics in the environment.* Keep the original caption, with an on-slide credit: **Source: Ajala et al. (2023), Fig. 1.**

## Slide 3 — WHY ARE ANTIBIOTIC RESIDUES CONCERNING?

1. **Environmental occurrence:** Antibiotic residues have been reported in surface water, groundwater, seawater, soil and wastewater-related matrices. (Gopal et al., 2020; Teo et al., 2022)
2. **Ecological effects:** Residues may affect aquatic organisms and microbial communities. (Gopal et al., 2020; Teo et al., 2022)
3. **Antimicrobial resistance:** Environmental exposure raises concerns about antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) and antibiotic-resistance genes (ARGs). (Teo et al., 2022; Ajala et al., 2023)
4. **Treatment challenge:** Conventional biological treatment may not remove antibiotic residues efficiently; incomplete removal has specifically been reported for tetracycline in conventional primary and secondary treatment. (Gopal et al., 2020; Teo et al., 2022)

**Key point:** Occurrence and treatment performance are antibiotic-, matrix- and process-specific. (Gopal et al., 2020; Teo et al., 2022)

**Suggested visual:** A simple pathway: use/discharge → residues in water → exposure of organisms and microbial communities → ecological and antimicrobial-resistance concerns. Credit the review sources in the slide footer.

## Slide 4 — TECHNOLOGIES FOR ANTIBIOTIC REMOVAL

| Technology | Main role | Important consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Biological treatment | Microorganisms treat wastewater. | Conventional biological treatment may remove antibiotic residues inefficiently. (Gopal et al., 2020; Teo et al., 2022) |
| Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) | Reactive oxidation transforms/degrades antibiotics. | Transformation products may remain; parent-compound disappearance is not automatically complete mineralisation or detoxification. (Gopal et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2024) |
| Membrane filtration / reverse osmosis | Separates contaminants from the water stream. | Fouling, energy demand and operating cost can limit use. (Gopal et al., 2020) |
| Adsorption | Transfers dissolved antibiotics from water to a solid. | The loaded adsorbent and captured antibiotic require recovery, regeneration and/or appropriate disposal. (Ajala et al., 2023; Teo et al., 2022) |

**Important:** Adsorption is a separation/retention process. It does not, by itself, establish destruction of the antibiotic molecule. (Ajala et al., 2023; Teo et al., 2022)

## Slide 5 — FUNDAMENTALS OF ADSORPTION

**Key terms**
- **Adsorbate:** dissolved antibiotic species being captured. (Ajala et al., 2023)
- **Adsorbent:** solid material providing the surface, pores and/or active sites. (Ajala et al., 2023; Teo et al., 2022)
- **Adsorption:** accumulation or retention at an interface. (Ajala et al., 2023)
- **Absorption:** uptake into the bulk of another phase. (Standard chemistry distinction; not presented as a specific result from the selected papers.)

**Main steps described for adsorption**
1. Movement from the bulk liquid towards the adsorbent.
2. Diffusion through the surrounding liquid film.
3. Movement towards accessible active sites.
4. Final adsorbate–adsorbent interaction. (Ajala et al., 2023)

**Pore filling** is a possible retention mode; it is not a fifth compulsory transport step. (Ajala et al., 2023)

**Suggested figure:** Use the supplied Ajala et al. (2023), Fig. 3 as the biomass-based sorption scheme, or draw an original four-step particle schematic. Caption: **Source: Ajala et al. (2023), Fig. 3.** Do not imply that every interaction shown applies to all adsorbents.

## Slide 6 — TYPES OF ADSORBENT MATERIALS

**1. Natural mineral adsorbents**
- Kaolinite and smectite-group minerals, including montmorillonite. (Perelomov et al., 2025)

**2. Non-biochar by-product sorbents**
- Raw date-palm waste. (Mirizadeh et al., 2023)
- Hemp waste, oak ash and uncalcined mussel shell. (Rodríguez-López et al., 2024)

**3. Carbonaceous adsorbents**
- Biochar, activated biochar and activated carbon. (Ajala et al., 2023; Teo et al., 2022)
- Feedstocks in the selected studies include cotton husk, sawdust, brewing-industry bagasse and antibiotic fermentation residue. (Chen et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024; Meseguer et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024)

**4. Engineered / modified adsorbents**
- Fe₂O₃-modified cotton-husk biochar (Fe₂O₃@BMBC). (Chen et al., 2024)

- ZnO-coated hybrid biochar. (Egbedina et al., 2021)

## Slide 7 — TYPES OF INTERACTIONS INVOLVED IN ANTIBIOTIC ADSORPTION

**Antibiotic molecules can be retained by adsorbents through several types of interactions. The contribution of each interaction depends on the antibiotic's chemical structure, its ionic form, the surface chemistry of the adsorbent and the solution conditions.** (Ajala et al., 2023; Teo et al., 2022; Perelomov et al., 2025)

**Main types of interactions**

1. **Electrostatic interactions:** Attraction or repulsion between charged antibiotic species and charged sites on the adsorbent surface. The direction and strength depend on antibiotic speciation and surface charge. (Teo et al., 2022; Perelomov et al., 2025)
2. **Hydrogen bonding:** Attraction involving suitable hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor groups on the antibiotic and adsorbent surface. (Ajala et al., 2023; Teo et al., 2022)
3. **π–π interactions:** Interactions between aromatic regions of an antibiotic molecule and aromatic domains on a carbonaceous surface. (Teo et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024)
4. **Hydrophobic interactions:** Association of relatively non-polar regions of the antibiotic with hydrophobic surface domains. (Teo et al., 2022)
5. **Ion exchange / cation bridging:** Exchange of antibiotic-associated ions with surface or interlayer cations, or bridging mediated by cations. These processes are particularly discussed for mineral surfaces and some composite systems. (Perelomov et al., 2025; Meseguer et al., 2024)
6. **Surface complexation / coordination:** Association of suitable antibiotic functional groups with surface metal-containing sites; Fe–O complexation is proposed for the Fe₂O₃@BMBC system. (Chen et al., 2024)

**Other retention pathway — pore filling:** Antibiotic molecules may enter and occupy accessible pores. Pore filling is a physical retention pathway, not a separate intermolecular force. (Ajala et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2024)

**Important:** These mechanisms may act together, but not every mechanism occurs in every adsorbent–antibiotic system. Their contribution must be supported by the relevant experimental evidence; a kinetic or isotherm fit alone does not establish a molecular mechanism. (Vareda, 2023; Teo et al., 2022)

**Suggested diagram (main visual):** Draw one general adsorbent surface with antibiotic molecules approaching it. Use separate callouts/arrows to illustrate: (a) electrostatic attraction, (b) hydrogen bonding, (c) π–π interaction between aromatic regions, (d) hydrophobic association, (e) ion exchange/cation bridging, and (f) surface complexation. Add a small pore cross-section showing pore filling as a separate retention pathway. Keep the diagram conceptual and label it **“Possible interactions and retention pathways”**—do not imply that all are present simultaneously.

## Slide 8 — FACTORS AFFECTING ADSORPTION

1. **pH and antibiotic speciation:** pH changes the ionic form of an antibiotic and can also change adsorbent surface charge. (Gopal et al., 2020; Perelomov et al., 2025)
2. **Adsorbent surface properties:** Surface charge, functional groups, pore structure and accessible sites influence uptake. (Teo et al., 2022; Perelomov et al., 2025)
3. **Adsorbent dosage:** Increasing dose changes the amount of available adsorbent relative to pollutant; removal may approach a plateau. (Liang et al., 2024; Meseguer et al., 2024)
4. **Initial antibiotic concentration:** Changes the solute-to-site ratio and can change uptake per gram and percentage removal differently. (Meseguer et al., 2024; Mirizadeh et al., 2023)
5. **Contact time:** Determines how long the system has to approach equilibrium. (Meseguer et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024)
6. **Temperature:** Can change equilibrium uptake; direction and magnitude are system-specific. (Meseguer et al., 2024; Mirizadeh et al., 2023)
7. **Ionic strength and competing ions:** Salt identity and concentration can alter electrostatic interactions and capacity. (Perelomov et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2024)
8. **Natural organic matter:** Humic substances can compete with antibiotics for adsorption sites. (Perelomov et al., 2025; Liang et al., 2024)
9. **Particle size / pore accessibility:** These affect preparation and access to adsorption sites, but the selected evidence does not establish a universal directional effect on antibiotic uptake. (Rodríguez-López et al., 2024; Teo et al., 2022)

**pKa and pHₚzc are not the same:** pKa describes antibiotic acid–base dissociation; pHₚzc is the pH at which the adsorbent's net surface charge is zero. (Gopal et al., 2020; Meseguer et al., 2024)

**Suggested figures:** Perelomov et al. (2025), Fig. 4 (clay-related factors); Liang et al. (2024), Fig. 4 (dose, zeta potential, pH, humic acid and ionic strength); Meseguer et al. (2024), Figs. 2–3 (pHₚzc and CPX pH effect). Use the actual graphs where possible.

## Slide 9 — EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE: FACTORS AFFECTING ADSORPTION

**NFO/BC + tetracycline**
- Increasing NFO/BC dose from 0.2 to 1.0 g L⁻¹ raised TC removal from 37% to 93.9% under the reported conditions. (Liang et al., 2024)
- Humic acid reduced TC removal. (Liang et al., 2024)
- NaCl had no significant effect in the reported test, whereas MgCl₂ and CaCl₂ reduced TC adsorption capacity. (Liang et al., 2024)

**K₂CO₃-activated bagasse biochar + ciprofloxacin**
- Blank experiments showed that apparent CPX disappearance at pH 7–9 was mainly due to precipitation, not adsorption. (Meseguer et al., 2024)
- The study used pH 3.5 in subsequent adsorption experiments to minimise precipitation. (Meseguer et al., 2024)
- Increasing initial concentration increased equilibrium uptake while percentage removal decreased. (Meseguer et al., 2024)
- Equilibrium uptake increased from 34.6 mg g⁻¹ at 10 °C to 109.9 mg g⁻¹ at 55 °C in this specific system. (Meseguer et al., 2024)
- About 82% of equilibrium uptake was reached after 24 h and equilibrium was reached at approximately 80 h under the stated conditions. (Meseguer et al., 2024)

**Suggested figures:** Liang et al. (2024), Fig. 4 and Meseguer et al. (2024), Fig. 3. Caption each separately with its source and original figure number.

## Slide 10 — ADSORPTION CAPACITY AND REMOVAL EFFICIENCY

**Equilibrium adsorption capacity**
[
q_e=\frac{(C_0-C_e)V}{m}
]

**Removal efficiency**
[
Y(\%)=\frac{C_0-C_e}{C_0}\times100
]

Where:
- (C_0) = initial concentration (mg L⁻¹)
- (C_e) = equilibrium concentration (mg L⁻¹)
- (V) = solution volume (L)
- (m) = adsorbent mass (g)
- (q_e) = equilibrium adsorption capacity (mg g⁻¹)

(Mirizadeh et al., 2023; Meseguer et al., 2024)

**Worked TC example — raw date-palm waste**
- (C_0=50) mg L⁻¹; (V=30) mL; dose = 1 g L⁻¹; (m=0.030) g.
- Reported (q_e=16.15) mg g⁻¹ at pH 10, 298 K and 3 h. (Mirizadeh et al., 2023)
- Derived (C_e=33.85) mg L⁻¹ and derived removal (Y=32.3%) from the reported uptake and dose. These are calculated values, not separately reported measurements. (Mirizadeh et al., 2023)

**Do not confuse:** uptake per gram, percentage removal, measured concentration and fitted maximum capacity.

**Suggested figure:** Meseguer et al. (2024), Fig. 6, which directly illustrates the differing trends of qₑ and percentage removal with initial concentration.

## Slide 11 — ADSORPTION ISOTHERMS

**An adsorption isotherm** describes the equilibrium relationship between adsorption capacity and the remaining dissolved concentration at a fixed temperature. (Ayawei et al., 2017)

| Model | Basic interpretation |
|---|---|
| Langmuir | Idealised finite equivalent sites and monolayer saturation. (Ayawei et al., 2017) |
| Freundlich | Empirical description of heterogeneous adsorption energies; no explicit finite saturation capacity. (Ayawei et al., 2017) |
| Temkin | Accounts for coverage-dependent change in adsorption heat. (Ayawei et al., 2017) |
| Sips | Hybrid form combining heterogeneity with finite saturation at high concentration. (Ayawei et al., 2017) |

**Examples from the selected studies**
- TC on raw date-palm waste: Langmuir was reported as the best fit; CIP was better described by D–R in the study's model comparison. (Mirizadeh et al., 2023)
- CIP on K₂CO₃-activated bagasse biochar: Freundlich, Redlich–Peterson and Sips gave good fits; Freundlich had the lowest reported ARE (2.44%). (Meseguer et al., 2024)
- TC on NFO/BC: Langmuir and Freundlich fits are shown and should be interpreted using the paper's reported fit statistics. (Liang et al., 2024)

**Caution:** A fitted isotherm is an empirical description of equilibrium data; it does not prove a molecular adsorption mechanism. (Ayawei et al., 2017; Vareda, 2023)

**Suggested figures:** Meseguer et al. (2024), Fig. 7; Liang et al. (2024), Fig. 5; Chen et al. (2024), Fig. 5. Use one or two plots and retain their original units/legends.

## Slide 12 — ADSORPTION KINETICS

**Kinetics** describes how adsorption uptake changes with contact time. (Vareda, 2023)

- (q_t): uptake at time (t).
- (q_e): uptake at equilibrium. (Vareda, 2023)

**Common kinetic models**
- Pseudo-first-order (PFO): describes uptake rate in relation to the remaining difference between (q_e) and (q_t). (Vareda, 2023)
- Pseudo-second-order (PSO): uses the square of that remaining uptake difference. (Vareda, 2023)
- Intraparticle diffusion: tests the relationship between uptake and (t^{1/2}). (Vareda, 2023)
- Elovich: empirical model used for adsorption on energetically heterogeneous surfaces. (Vareda, 2023)

**Examples**
- CIP on activated bagasse biochar: Elovich and Bangham gave the lowest reported average relative errors (ARE < 3.2%). (Meseguer et al., 2024)
- TC on NFO/BC: PFO and PSO fits are reported; interpret them as empirical fits. (Liang et al., 2024)
- OTC/CIP on Fe₂O₃@BMBC: PFO/PSO fits and intraparticle-diffusion plots are presented. (Chen et al., 2024)

**Important:** A better PFO or PSO fit alone does not prove chemisorption or identify the unique rate-controlling step. (Vareda, 2023)

**Suggested figures:** Meseguer et al. (2024), Figs. 4–5; Liang et al. (2024), Fig. 6; Chen et al. (2024), Fig. 4.

## Slide 13 — THERMODYNAMICS OF ADSORPTION

**Thermodynamic quantities**
- (Delta G): Gibbs free-energy change; indicates favourability under the authors' thermodynamic convention.
- (Delta H): enthalpy change; positive indicates endothermic and negative indicates exothermic behaviour.
- (Delta S): entropy change for the defined system. (Liang et al., 2024; Meseguer et al., 2024; Mirizadeh et al., 2023)

**Relationships**
[
Delta G=-RT\ln K_d
]
[
Delta G=Delta H-TDelta S
]
[
\ln K_d=-\frac{\Delta H}{RT}+\frac{\Delta S}{R}
]
(Liang et al., 2024)

**Temperature example:** CIP uptake on K₂CO₃-activated bagasse biochar increased from 34.6 mg g⁻¹ at 10 °C to 109.9 mg g⁻¹ at 55 °C in the reported experiment. (Meseguer et al., 2024)

**Caution:** Temperature response is system-specific; it should not be used alone to calculate or claim thermodynamic parameters. (Meseguer et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024)

## Slide 14 — REGENERATION AND REUSABILITY

**A regeneration cycle**
Adsorption → separate loaded solid → desorb antibiotic into regenerant → rinse / neutralise / dry → test next adsorption cycle.

**Terms**
- Desorption = release of antibiotic from the solid into a liquid regenerant. (Gkika et al., 2025)
- Regeneration = treatment of spent adsorbent to restore usable adsorption function. (Gkika et al., 2025)
- Reuse = placing regenerated solid into another adsorption run.
- Capacity retention compares capacity after cycle (n) with fresh capacity.

[
\text{Capacity retention (\%)}=\frac{q_n}{q_{\mathrm{fresh}}}\times100
]

**Selected evidence**
- Fe₂O₃@BMBC: after five cycles, 86.8% OTC and 91.1% CIP capacity retained using ethanol + ultrasound co-processing. (Chen et al., 2024)
- NFO/BC: 72% TC removal after cycle 4; this is cycle removal, not a reported capacity-retention percentage. (Liang et al., 2024)
- Fermentation-residue biochar: slight decline after five cycles using NaOH regeneration; exact final retention not stated. (Zhao et al., 2024)

**Important:** The regenerant and wash streams contain released antibiotic and require suitable management. (Gkika et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024)

**Suggested figures:** Chen et al. (2024), Fig. 7; Liang et al. (2024), Fig. 4(f); Meseguer et al. (2024) does not report completed regeneration cycles and should not be shown as reuse evidence.

## Slide 15 — LOW-COST POTENTIAL AND PRACTICAL CHALLENGES

**The complete cost chain**
Feedstock → collection / transport / drying → preparation → activation / modification → adsorption → solid recovery → regeneration → secondary-waste management. (Kyzas & Kostoglou, 2014; Gkika et al., 2025)

**Cost units**
- €/kg adsorbent = cost to make or buy a mass of adsorbent.
- €/m³ treated = cost to treat a volume of water.
- Fresh adsorbent contribution to treatment-volume cost = adsorbent unit cost × dose (kg m⁻³). (Kyzas & Kostoglou, 2014; Gkika et al., 2025)

**Practical questions**
1. Does it work in the relevant wastewater matrix? (Teo et al., 2022; Perelomov et al., 2025)
2. Can the adsorbent be recovered from water? (Chen et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024)
3. Does it retain useful capacity after regeneration? (Chen et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024)
4. What are the total preparation, energy, chemical, recovery and waste-management costs? (Kyzas & Kostoglou, 2014; Gkika et al., 2025)

**Evidence gap:** The selected antibiotic studies do not provide directly comparable full production costs or cost per cubic metre of antibiotic wastewater treated. (Chen et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024; Meseguer et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024)

## Slide 16 — CONCLUSIONS

1. **Adsorption performance is system-specific.** Antibiotic speciation, adsorbent chemistry, pore access and water matrix all matter. (Perelomov et al., 2025; Teo et al., 2022)
2. **Adsorption is separation, not degradation.** Transfer to a solid does not establish destruction of the antibiotic. (Ajala et al., 2023; Teo et al., 2022)
3. **Metrics are not interchangeable.** Uptake, removal percentage, fitted capacity and capacity retention answer different questions. (Ayawei et al., 2017; Vareda, 2023; Mirizadeh et al., 2023)
4. **Reuse requires a complete process.** Recovery, regeneration, retained capacity and regenerant management must be considered. (Gkika et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024)
5. **Waste-derived does not automatically mean low-cost.** Preparation, energy, chemicals, dose, recovery and waste management must be included. (Kyzas & Kostoglou, 2014; Gkika et al., 2025)

**Take-home:** Practical low-cost adsorption requires relevant-water performance + recoverability + whole-process economics.
