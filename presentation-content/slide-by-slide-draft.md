# Slide 4 — Treatment Technologies for Antibiotic Removal

### Learning objective

Compare the roles and selected limitations of biological treatment, advanced oxidation, membrane processes and adsorption.

### On-slide content

| Technology | Main role | Important consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Biological treatment | Microorganisms treat wastewater | Conventional biological treatment may remove antibiotic residues inefficiently |
| Advanced oxidation (AOPs) | Reactive oxidation transforms/degrades antibiotics | Oxidised by-products may be toxic; transformation is not automatically mineralisation |
| Membrane filtration / reverse osmosis | Separates contaminants from the water stream | Fouling, energy demand and operating cost can limit use; reverse osmosis has additional reported limitations |
| Adsorption | Transfers antibiotics from water to a solid | The loaded adsorbent and captured antibiotic require recovery, regeneration and/or appropriate disposal |

**Adsorption separates the antibiotic from water; it does not, by itself, establish destruction of the antibiotic molecule.**

### Visual specification

A four-column technology comparison with a small icon per process. At the bottom show: antibiotic-containing water → contact with adsorbent → treated liquid + antibiotic-loaded solid. Label the solid stream “recover / regenerate / dispose appropriately”. Add a small AOP note: “check transformation products and toxicity”.

### Presenter notes

The selected sources describe several approaches to antibiotic removal, but they do not provide a universal ranking across compounds, water matrices or treatment installations.

Biological treatment remains a wastewater-treatment route, but Teo et al. (2022) describe conventional biological treatment as inefficient for antibiotic residues. Gopal et al. (2020) specifically report that primary and secondary treatment were insufficient for complete tetracycline (TC) removal in the settings reviewed. Keep that example TC-specific.

AOPs use oxidative chemistry to transform/degrade antibiotics. The sources caution that oxidised by-products may be toxic or more toxic; disappearance of the parent antibiotic should not automatically be described as complete mineralisation or detoxification.

Gopal et al. (2020) report membrane fouling, high energy requirements and high operating costs as limitations for nanofiltration/ultrafiltration; they also describe fouling, high operational cost and limitations at residual antibiotic concentrations for reverse osmosis. Avoid making a universal cost ranking across all membrane installations.

Adsorption transfers chemical substances from the liquid phase to the solid phase. It is therefore a separation/retention process, not proof of degradation. The loaded adsorbent and captured antibiotic require a recovery, regeneration and/or disposal strategy.

# Slide 5 — Fundamentals of Adsorption

### Learning objective

Explain the adsorbate–adsorbent system and the principal steps by which dissolved molecules reach adsorption sites.

### On-slide content

**Key terms**
- **Adsorbate:** dissolved antibiotic species being captured.
- **Adsorbent:** solid material providing the surface, pores and/or active sites.
- **Adsorption:** accumulation or retention at an interface.
- **Absorption:** uptake into the bulk of another phase.

**Principal steps described in the review**

Bulk water → movement toward the adsorbent → diffusion through the surrounding liquid film → movement toward accessible active sites → adsorbate–adsorbent interaction

*Pore filling is one possible adsorption mode, not an additional compulsory step.*

### Visual specification

Draw an original porous adsorbent particle with antibiotic molecules in bulk water, a liquid boundary film and arrows showing the four-step sequence: movement from bulk water toward the particle; diffusion across the liquid film; movement toward accessible active sites; and final interaction at the surface. Show pore filling as a separate, labelled callout (“possible retention mode”), not as a universal transport stage. Add a small inset contrasting surface accumulation with uptake into the material’s bulk.

### Presenter notes

Ajala et al. (2023) describe four principal steps: movement of adsorbate molecules from the bulk matrix toward the adsorbent, diffusion through the liquid film surrounding the adsorbent particle, movement toward active sites, and a final interaction with the adsorbent surface (PDF p. 9; journal p. 4686). The review's Figure 3 separately illustrates possible modes such as pore filling, electrostatic interaction, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interaction and surface complexation (PDF p. 16; journal p. 4693). Pore filling should therefore be shown as a possible mode, not inserted as a mandatory fifth transport step.

The four-step passage in Ajala et al. appears to contain the phrase “adsorbent–adsorbent interactions”. In context, the passage concerns antibiotic molecules (the adsorbate) reaching sites on the adsorbent; the slide therefore uses “adsorbate–adsorbent interaction” as an editorial clarification, not as a verbatim quotation.

The adsorption-versus-absorption distinction is standard chemistry teaching terminology. The inspected passages in the fixed papers do not provide a dedicated formal comparison of those terms. Rodríguez-López et al. (2024) separately distinguish adsorption and biosorption in terms of the sorbent's biological origin; this is a different distinction.

Stress that adsorption retains the antibiotic on a solid phase; this alone does not prove degradation or mineralisation.

### Source citations

- Ajala et al. (2023), PDF p. 9 / journal p. 4686 — four principal steps in the adsorption sequence.
- Ajala et al. (2023), Figure 3, PDF p. 16 / journal p. 4693 — illustrative adsorption modes, including pore filling.
- Rodríguez-López et al. (2024), PDF p. 2 — adsorption/biosorption distinction based on sorbent origin.

# Slide 8 — Factors Affecting Adsorption

### Learning objective

Explain how antibiotic speciation, adsorbent properties and water-matrix conditions influence adsorption, and why the effects are system-specific.

### On-slide content

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **pH** | Changes antibiotic speciation and adsorbent surface charge; their interaction can cause attraction or repulsion |
| **Adsorbent properties** | Surface charge, functional groups and accessible pore structure influence available sites and interactions |
| **Adsorbent dose** | Changes the amount of available adsorbent relative to pollutant; removal may approach a plateau |
| **Initial concentration and contact time** | Change the solute-to-site ratio and the time available to approach equilibrium |
| **Ionic strength / competing ions** | Different ions can alter charge effects or compete for adsorption sites |
| **Natural organic matter** | May compete with antibiotics for adsorption sites |
| **Temperature** | Can change equilibrium uptake; the direction and size of the effect depend on the system |

**Tetracycline example:** Gopal et al. (2020) report pKa values of approximately 3.3, 7.7 and 9.7. The dominant species changes progressively as pH increases.

*Experimental caution:* Check for precipitation before interpreting disappearance from solution as adsorption. In one ciprofloxacin–activated-biochar study, much of the apparent removal at pH 7–9 was precipitation.

### Visual specification

Left: reproduce an original, simplified TC speciation band based on Gopal et al. (2020), with pKa markers at 3.3, 7.7 and 9.7. Show progressive deprotonation: cationic at low pH, zwitterionic/intermediate forms, and anionic forms at alkaline pH. Mark pKa3 at 9.7 as further deprotonation in the alkaline range; do not imply that all speciation changes occur at one sharp boundary.

Add a small separate inset showing that **antibiotic pKa** and **adsorbent pHₚzc** are different properties. Right: a factor wheel around an adsorbent particle, with separate labels for ions and natural organic matter. Add a small “experimental caution” note: apparent CPX removal at pH 7–9 in Meseguer et al. (2024) was mainly precipitation, not adsorption.

### Presenter notes

pH affects both the ionic form of the antibiotic and the charge of the adsorbent. These properties must be considered together. Antibiotic pKa values describe acid–base dissociation of the molecule; the adsorbent's point of zero charge (pHₚzc) describes the pH at which its net surface charge is zero. They are not interchangeable.

**Tetracycline speciation.** Gopal et al. (2020) report TC pKa values of approximately 3.3, 7.7 and 9.7 and provide a species-distribution diagram (PDF pp. 2–3; journal pp. 27082–27083). Their simplified text describes cationic TC below pH 3.3, zwitterionic TC between pH 3.3 and 7.7, and anionic TC above pH 7.7. Retain the third pKa at 9.7 as a further alkaline deprotonation; do not omit it or imply a single sharp switch in the full distribution.

**Different antibiotics and adsorbents have different pH behaviour.** Perelomov et al. (2025) report OTC pKa values of 3.57, 7.49 and 9.88 and discuss a maximum kaolinite adsorption capacity near pH 5.5 for that particular OTC–kaolinite system (PDF p. 16). In Liang et al. (2024), the NFO/BC pHₚzc was approximately 5.35; TC removal was highest (92.4%) at pH 7.0 under the tested conditions (PDF p. 7). Do not transfer either optimum to other adsorbent–antibiotic pairs.

**Precipitation must be separated from adsorption.** Meseguer et al. (2024) report that CPX was predominantly zwitterionic between pH 5.9 and 8.9 and had low solubility in that form. In their activated-bagasse-biochar study, turbidity and blank tests showed that apparent removal at pH 7–9 was mainly caused by CPX precipitation rather than adsorption (PDF p. 8). The authors used pH 3.5 in subsequent tests to minimise precipitation. They report a pHₚzc of approximately 8.1 for the activated biochar (PDF p. 7). At pH 3.5, CPX is cationic while the biochar surface is positively charged; the authors therefore state that electrostatic contribution should be slight and suggest hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions as possible contributors (PDF p. 7). Do not present the pH 7–9 disappearance as adsorption performance.

**Dose and matrix effects.** In the NFO/BC–TC study, increasing dose from 0.2 to 1.0 g L⁻¹ raised removal from 37% to 93.9%; further increase gave little additional benefit, while adsorbent cost increased (Liang et al., 2024, PDF p. 7). Increasing humic-acid dosage reduced TC removal. NaCl had no significant effect in the reported tests, whereas MgCl₂ and CaCl₂ reduced capacity; at the reported 0.5% concentration, capacity fell to 59.8% and 63.8% of the original value, respectively (PDF p. 8). Perelomov et al. (2025) also report decreasing tetracycline adsorption with increasing ionic strength and reduced OTC sorption onto clay in the presence of high dissolved organic matter (PDF p. 16). These findings show that salt identity, matrix composition and adsorbent type matter.

**Temperature and contact time are also system-dependent.** For CIP on K₂CO₃-activated bagasse biochar, equilibrium uptake increased from 34.6 mg g⁻¹ at 10 °C to 109.9 mg g⁻¹ at 55 °C (Meseguer et al., 2024, PDF p. 8). About 82% of equilibrium uptake was reached after 24 h, with equilibrium at approximately 80 h under the stated conditions (PDF pp. 1 and 5). This is one experimental system, not a universal temperature or contact-time rule.

Initial concentration changes the solute-to-site ratio and is varied in the selected adsorption experiments. Particle size is reported as a preparation/operating parameter in Rodríguez-López et al. (2024, PDF p. 2), but that cited passage does not establish a universal directional effect of particle size on antibiotic uptake.

Do not claim that increasing dose, temperature, time or ionic strength always improves adsorption. Report the material, antibiotic and conditions whenever giving a directional trend.

### Source citations

- Gopal et al. (2020), PDF pp. 2–3 — TC pKa values and species-distribution diagram.
- Perelomov et al. (2025), PDF pp. 5–7 and p. 16 — clay surface properties, OTC pKa/pH example and ionic-strength/dissolved-organic-matter effects.
- Rodríguez-López et al. (2024), PDF p. 2 — particle-size preparation and operational parameters.

# Slide 9 — Adsorption Capacity and Removal Efficiency

### Learning objective

Calculate the mass of antibiotic retained per gram of adsorbent and the percentage removed from water.

### On-slide content

**Equilibrium adsorption capacity — constant-volume batch system**

\[
q_e=\frac{(C_0-C_e)V}{m}
\]

**Removal efficiency**

\[
Y(\%)=\frac{C_0-C_e}{C_0}\times100
\]

Where \(C_0\) and \(C_e\) are initial and equilibrium concentrations (mg L⁻¹), \(V\) is solution volume (L), \(m\) is adsorbent mass (g), and \(q_e\) is uptake (mg g⁻¹).

**Worked example — Mirizadeh et al. (2023), tetracycline on raw date-palm fibre**
- Conditions: pH 10; 298 K; contact time 3 h
- \(C_0=50\) mg L⁻¹
- \(V=30\) mL; dose = 1 g L⁻¹; therefore \(m=0.030\) g
- Reported \(q_e=16.15\) mg g⁻¹
- Derived \(C_e=33.85\) mg L⁻¹
- Derived \(Y=32.3\%\)

*Back-calculation note:* \(C_e=33.85\) mg L⁻¹ and \(Y=32.3\%\) are reconstructed from the reported \(q_e\) and dose. REF-09 Figure 1 also plots removal efficiency for the pH series, but the article text does not state an exact numerical \(Y\) value for pH 10.

### Visual specification

Left: known solution + known adsorbent mass → controlled conditions → solid/liquid separation → residual concentration measurement. Right: equations and symbol definitions. Bottom strip labels each number “reported” or “derived”.

### Presenter notes

Capacity and removal percentage describe different things. Capacity is the amount retained per mass of adsorbent; removal percentage is the fractional decrease in liquid concentration. High percentage removal does not necessarily mean high capacity per gram.

Mirizadeh et al. (2023) report the TC uptake of 16.15 mg g⁻¹ at \(C_0=50\) mg L⁻¹, dose 1 g L⁻¹, volume 30 mL, pH 10 and 298 K, with 3 h contact time (PDF pp. 2–3). The dose and volume imply \(m=0.030\) g. The adsorbed mass is \(16.15\times0.030=0.4845\) mg. The concentration decrease is \(0.4845/0.030=16.15\) mg L⁻¹, so derived \(C_e=33.85\) mg L⁻¹ and derived removal \(=(50-33.85)/50\times100=32.3\%\).

Use \(q_e\) only for uptake at equilibrium; at a specified earlier time, use \(q_t\). Meseguer et al. (2024) use a volume-aware mass balance, \(q_t=(C_0V_0-C_tV_t)/m\), when liquid volume changes during sampling. Zhao et al. (2024) prints \((C_e-C_0)\) in its capacity/removal equations despite defining \(C_0\) as initial and \(C_e\) as equilibrium concentration; do not reproduce that sign as the positive uptake convention.

- Mirizadeh et al. (2023), PDF p. 3, Figure 1 and Section 3.1 — reported uptake and conditions.

# Slide 10 — Adsorption Isotherms

### Learning objective

Understand how equilibrium uptake depends on residual concentration and how common models describe that relationship.

### On-slide content

An **adsorption isotherm** relates equilibrium uptake \(q_e\) to equilibrium liquid concentration \(C_e\) at specified temperature and solution conditions.

**Langmuir**

\[
q_e=\frac{q_{mL}K_LC_e}{1+K_LC_e}
\]

Finite fitted capacity; idealised monolayer model.

**Freundlich**

\[
q_e=K_FC_e^{1/n}
\]

Empirical heterogeneous-surface model; no explicit finite \(q_m\).

**Temkin**

\[
q_e=\frac{RT}{b_T}\ln(K_TC_e)
\]

Assumes a coverage-related decrease in adsorption heat.

**Sips**

\[
q_e=\frac{q_{mS}K_SC_e^{1/n_S}}
{1+K_SC_e^{1/n_S}}
\]

Combines Freundlich-like low-concentration behaviour with Langmuir-like high-concentration saturation.

**Examples: the best fit depends on the antibiotic and adsorbent.**
- Mirizadeh et al. (2023): Langmuir best for TC; D–R best for CIP.
- Egbedina et al. (2021): best model varies across CIP/TET and KCB material treatments.
- Meseguer et al. (2024): Freundlich best for CIP on activated bagasse biochar (ARE = 2.44%).

### Visual specification

Four conceptual panels: Langmuir plateau; Freundlich heterogeneous curve without finite saturation parameter; Temkin logarithmic relationship; Sips heterogeneous-to-saturating curve. Label all model curves “schematic”. Add a compact study-comparison strip.

### Presenter notes

An isotherm is an equilibrium relation, not a rate equation. It does not show how long equilibrium takes to establish. \(q_{mL}\) and \(q_{mS}\) are fitted capacities. Freundlich has no explicit finite saturation-capacity parameter, so \(K_F\) must not be called a maximum capacity. Units for several constants depend on concentration and exponent conventions.

Mirizadeh et al. (2023) report Langmuir as the best tested model for TC (\(R^2=0.977\)) and D–R as best for CIP (\(R^2=0.975\)); the authors interpret the TC Langmuir fit using the idealised homogeneous-site/monolayer description (PDF pp. 6–7). Egbedina et al. (2021) show model selection varies with antibiotic and activation state (PDF pp. 5–7). Meseguer et al. (2024) fit nonlinear equations and identify Freundlich as best by the lowest ARE (2.44%); the measured curve does not reach a clear plateau in the tested range (PDF pp. 6 and 11–12).

Ayawei et al. (2017) explain that linearisation changes the error structure and recommends nonlinear regression on original equations; model selection should not rely on \(R^2\) alone. The Sips equation shown follows Meseguer et al. (2024)'s parameterisation. Ayawei et al. (2017) prints a different denominator sign/parameterisation; the source discrepancy is preserved in the extraction.

A good isotherm fit is a statistical description under particular conditions, not proof of a unique molecular mechanism.

# Slide 11 — Adsorption Kinetics

### Learning objective

Explain uptake over time, compare common kinetic models and understand the limits of mechanistic inference.

### On-slide content

**Kinetics describes uptake as a function of contact time.**

**Pseudo-first-order (PFO)**

\[
q_t=q_e\left(1-e^{-k_1t}\right)
\]

**Pseudo-second-order (PSO)**

\[
q_t=\frac{k_2q_e^2t}{1+k_2q_et}
\]

**Weber–Morris intraparticle diffusion**

\[
q_t=k_{\mathrm{WM}}t^{1/2}+C
\]

**Elovich**

\[
q_t=\frac{1}{\beta}\ln(1+\alpha\beta t)
\]

**Contact time differs across studied systems**
- Mirizadeh et al. (2023): about 120 min for TC and 90 min for CIP on raw date-palm fibre.
- Egbedina et al. (2021): most uptake in the first 15 min for the tested hybrid biochars.
- Meseguer et al. (2024): more than 3 days to reach equilibrium for CIP on activated bagasse biochar.

*These are not head-to-head results; conditions and materials differ.*

### Visual specification

Top: illustrative \(q_t\)-versus-time curve with rapid initial uptake and a later plateau. Label “schematic”. Bottom-left: four equation cards. Bottom-right: bulk liquid → boundary film → external surface → intraparticle pores → adsorption sites.

### Presenter notes

For constant volume, time-dependent uptake is \(q_t=(C_0-C_t)V/m\). If liquid volume changes during sampling, use the volume-aware mass balance. Vareda (2023) describes bulk transport, film diffusion, pore diffusion and surface interaction as relevant steps (PDF p. 2).

PFO and PSO are empirical kinetic descriptions. A better PSO fit alone does not prove chemisorption: Vareda (2023) explains that PSO can fit surface-reaction-controlled and diffusion-controlled systems. Linearisation can bias model selection; the review recommends nonlinear regression on original equations and discusses AIC and BIC (PDF pp. 4–9). A Weber–Morris nonzero intercept or multiple linear regions indicates that intraparticle diffusion alone does not describe the entire uptake curve under the model assumptions. Vareda (2023) describes Elovich as most useful far from equilibrium and notes limitations at long times (PDF p. 6).

Mirizadeh et al. (2023) report about 120 min for TC and 90 min for CIP (PDF p. 3). Egbedina et al. (2021) report most uptake within 15 min for its hybrid biochars (PDF p. 7). Meseguer et al. (2024) report more than three days to equilibrium for its activated bagasse/CIP system (PDF p. 9). Do not rank the materials by these times without matching conditions.

### Source citations

- Vareda (2023), PDF pp. 2–6 — transport sequence and kinetic models.
- Vareda (2023), PDF pp. 7–9 — linearisation bias and model-selection cautions.
- Mirizadeh et al. (2023), PDF pp. 3 and 5–6 — contact time and kinetics.
- Egbedina et al. (2021), PDF pp. 7–8 — hybrid-biochar kinetics.
- Meseguer et al. (2024), PDF pp. 9–10 — contact time and model comparison.

# Slide 12 — Thermodynamics of Adsorption

### Learning objective

Interpret thermodynamic signs used to discuss favourability and heat effects during adsorption.

### On-slide content

**Three thermodynamic quantities**
- \(\Delta G^\circ\): Gibbs free-energy change
- \(\Delta H^\circ\): enthalpy change
- \(\Delta S^\circ\): entropy change

**Relationships reported in Liang et al. (2024)**

\[
\Delta G^\circ=-RT\ln K_d
\]

\[
\Delta G^\circ=\Delta H^\circ-T\Delta S^\circ
\]

\[
\ln K_d=-\frac{\Delta H^\circ}{RT}+\frac{\Delta S^\circ}{R}
\]

*The \(K_d\) notation is reproduced from Liang et al. (2024); its standard-state normalisation is not clear in the supplied main text.*

**Sign interpretation**
- \(\Delta G^\circ<0\): spontaneous/favourable under the stated conditions
- \(\Delta H^\circ>0\): endothermic
- \(\Delta H^\circ<0\): exothermic

**Temperature-response example:** For CIP on K₂CO₃-activated bagasse biochar, reported \(q_e\) increased from 34.6 to 109.9 mg g⁻¹ between 10 and 55 °C.

### Visual specification

Left: the three quantities with sign labels. Right: a Van't Hoff plot schematic of \(\ln K\) versus \(1/T\), with slope \(=-\Delta H^\circ/R\) and intercept \(=\Delta S^\circ/R\). Label “relationship, not experimental data”. Bottom: four-point temperature-response plot from Meseguer et al. (2024).

### Presenter notes

Thermodynamics addresses favourability and heat effects, not rate. A spontaneous process need not be fast. A positive enthalpy indicates an endothermic process; it does not independently prove chemisorption.

Liang et al. (2024) report negative \(\Delta G\) for TC adsorption on BC, NFO and NFO/BC, interpreted as spontaneous. It describes TC adsorption on NFO as exothermic and on BC/NFO-BC as endothermic, and explicitly reports \(\Delta H=+31.384\) kJ mol⁻¹ for NFO/BC (PDF pp. 9–10). Table S4 is not in the supplied PDF. The main text also does not clearly define the standard-state normalisation of \(K_d\). The displayed equations reproduce the paper's notation; in thermodynamic logarithms the equilibrium constant must be dimensionless, so do not independently calculate \(\Delta G^\circ\) from a dimensional distribution coefficient without a justified standard-state conversion.

Meseguer et al. (2024) report CIP uptake of 34.6, 41.4, 89.3 and 109.9 mg g⁻¹ at 10, 25, 40 and 55 °C (PDF p. 9). Conditions: pH 3.5, 0.025 g adsorbent, 50 mL of 300 mg L⁻¹ solution and 72 h contact time (PDF p. 5). The authors interpret the trend as endothermic; the main text does not give a numerical \(\Delta H^\circ\) table.

Mirizadeh et al. (2023) qualitatively report endothermicity from its missing Supporting Information. Zhao et al. (2024) report increasing temperature-dependent fitted Langmuir capacities and interpret the result as endothermic. Keep fitted \(q_m\) distinct from enthalpy.

- Meseguer et al. (2024), PDF pp. 5 and 9 — temperature conditions and uptake.

# Slide 13 — Regeneration and Reusability

### Learning objective

Distinguish desorption from restored adsorption performance and assess whether an adsorbent can be reused.

### On-slide content

**Regeneration cycle**

Adsorption → solid/liquid separation → desorption/regeneration → rinse/dry → next adsorption cycle

**Do not confuse these measures**
- **Desorption efficiency:** how much adsorbed antibiotic is released.
- **Regeneration performance:** how well the material adsorbs again after treatment.
- **Capacity retention:** reused capacity relative to fresh capacity.

**Selected cycle evidence**

| Study | Regeneration procedure | Reported reuse result |
|---|---|---|
| Chen et al. (2024): OTC/CIP on magnetic cotton-husk biochar | Ethanol + ultrasound | After 5 cycles: 86.8% OTC and 91.1% CIP capacity retained |
| Liang et al. (2024): TC on NiFe₂O₄/biochar | DI-water desorption in the reusability test | 72% TC removal reported after cycle 4 |
| Zhao et al. (2024): TC on fermentation-residue biochar | 0.1 M NaOH, rinse to pH 7, dry | 5 cycles; authors report only a slight capacity decrease |

### Visual specification

A circular process diagram for adsorption → separation → desorption → rinse/dry → reuse. Place the three-study comparison beside it. Label Liang et al. (2024)'s value as “removal after cycle 4”, not “capacity retention”.

### Presenter notes

Desorption alone does not establish restored adsorption performance. Gkika et al. (2025) explain that a desorption test does not measure performance in the following adsorption cycle; regeneration performance requires a subsequent adsorption test (PDF p. 7).

Chen et al. (2024) report 86.8% OTC and 91.1% CIP capacity remaining after five ethanol/ultrasound cycles; its water-washing control retained 21.2% and 19.7%, respectively (PDF pp. 3–4 and p. 10, Figure 7). Liang et al. (2024) report a 72% TC removal rate after its fourth reuse cycle (PDF p. 8), not explicitly 72% of initial capacity retained. Zhao et al. (2024) use 0.1 M NaOH for five cycles and reports only a slight capacity decrease, without a precise final retention percentage in the text (PDF p. 5 and pp. 13–14).

Rodríguez-López et al. (2024) report antibiotic desorption/release from hemp waste, oak ash and mussel shell, but not a multi-cycle regeneration test (PDF pp. 3–6). Meseguer et al. (2024) calls for further regeneration/reuse work rather than reporting completed cycles in its supplied main paper (PDF p. 13).

Regeneration creates a liquid stream containing desorbed antibiotic. Eluent, wash water, energy, drying, adsorbent loss and possible leaching all affect feasibility.

# Slide 14 — Low-Cost Potential and Practical Challenges

### Learning objective

Evaluate whether a waste-derived adsorbent is cost-effective across the whole treatment process.

### On-slide content

**The real cost chain**

Feedstock → preparation → activation/modification → adsorption operation → recovery → regeneration → residual-waste management

**Cost drivers**
- Feedstock collection, transport, storage and drying
- Reagents, pyrolysis, inert gas, milling and synthesis
- Adsorbent dose, contact time, mixing and separation
- Eluent, washing, drying and solvent recovery
- Treatment of antibiotic-loaded liquids and spent solids

**Practical questions**
- Does it work in real wastewater?
- Can the solid be recovered efficiently?
- Does capacity remain useful after regeneration?
- What is the total cost per m³ treated?

**Evidence gap:** The selected antibiotic studies do not provide comparable full production costs or costs per volume of antibiotic wastewater treated.

### Visual specification

A process-cost pipeline with a “hidden costs” band: energy | chemicals | water | recovery | regeneration | waste handling. Include the distinction: €/kg adsorbent ≠ €/m³ treated water.

### Presenter notes

Waste-derived feedstock does not automatically mean a low-cost treatment process. Kyzas and Kostoglou (2014) identifies equipment, adsorbent consumption, electricity, regeneration/reactivation and spent-adsorbent disposal as cost elements; it warns that a low-cost adsorbent that is difficult to regenerate may not be economical (PDF pp. 19–20).

The antibiotic studies describe process requirements but do not provide comparable full cost calculations. Meseguer et al. (2024) includes drying, argon pyrolysis, K₂CO₃ activation, an 800 °C treatment, washing and further drying (PDF pp. 2–4). Zhao et al. (2024) use high-moisture fermentation residue, KOH activation, nitrogen pyrolysis, acid washing, water rinsing and vacuum drying (PDF pp. 2–4). Chen et al. (2024)'s magnetic composite requires high-temperature pyrolysis, iron precursor and ball milling; its ethanol/ultrasound regeneration uses substantial liquid volumes relative to adsorbent mass (PDF pp. 3–4). Liang et al. (2024) use nickel and iron salts, ammonia adjustment, hydrothermal processing, centrifugation and repeated washing (PDF pp. 3–4).

Gkika et al. (2025) provide a useful cost-accounting framework and numerical examples, but many cases concern non-antibiotic pollutants such as ibuprofen and BPA. Do not present these figures as antibiotic-treatment costs. The source also contains an unresolved difference between one Table 5 cost entry and the summary range in its conclusion.

A cost per kilogram of adsorbent cannot be directly compared with a cost per litre or cubic metre of treated water. Treatment cost depends on dose, influent and target concentrations, working capacity, recovery and regeneration. Meseguer et al. (2024) explicitly calls for real-effluent and regeneration/reuse work (PDF p. 13).

# Slide 15 — Conclusion and References

### Learning objective

Summarise the scientific lessons and identify the main research and engineering questions.

### On-slide content

**Key conclusions**

1. Antibiotic adsorption depends on antibiotic speciation, adsorbent surface chemistry, pore structure and water-matrix conditions.
2. Capacity, removal efficiency, isotherm fit, kinetic fit and thermodynamic favourability describe different aspects of performance.
3. Regeneration and reliable adsorbent recovery are essential to practical reuse.
4. Waste-derived does not automatically mean low-cost: preparation, operation, regeneration and disposal costs must be assessed.

**Final message**

*An effective low-cost adsorbent must remove the target antibiotic, remain recoverable and reusable, and demonstrate acceptable performance and cost in realistic water-treatment conditions.*
