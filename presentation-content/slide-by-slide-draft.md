# Slide 4 — Treatment Technologies for Antibiotic Removal

### Learning objective

Compare the roles and selected limitations of biological treatment, advanced oxidation, membrane processes and adsorption.

### On-slide content

| Technology | Main role | Important consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Biological treatment | Microorganisms treat wastewater | Conventional biological treatment may remove antibiotic residues inefficiently |
| Advanced oxidation (AOPs) | Reactive oxidation transforms/degrades antibiotics | Oxidised by-products may be toxic; transformation is not automatically mineralisation |
| Membrane filtration / reverse osmosis | Separates contaminants from the water stream | Fouling, energy demand and operating cost can limit use; reverse osmosis has additional reported limitations |
| Adsorption | Transfers antibiotics from water to a solid | The loaded adsorbent and captured antibiotic require recovery, regeneration and/or appropriate disposal |

**Adsorption separates the antibiotic from water; it does not, by itself, establish destruction of the antibiotic molecule.**

### Visual specification

A four-column technology comparison with a small icon per process. At the bottom show: antibiotic-containing water → contact with adsorbent → treated liquid + antibiotic-loaded solid. Label the solid stream “recover / regenerate / dispose appropriately”. Add a small AOP note: “check transformation products and toxicity”.

### Presenter notes

The selected sources describe several approaches to antibiotic removal, but they do not provide a universal ranking across compounds, water matrices or treatment installations.

Biological treatment remains a wastewater-treatment route, but Teo et al. (2022) describe conventional biological treatment as inefficient for antibiotic residues. Gopal et al. (2020) specifically report that primary and secondary treatment were insufficient for complete tetracycline (TC) removal in the settings reviewed. Keep that example TC-specific.

AOPs use oxidative chemistry to transform/degrade antibiotics. The sources caution that oxidised by-products may be toxic or more toxic; disappearance of the parent antibiotic should not automatically be described as complete mineralisation or detoxification.

Gopal et al. (2020) report membrane fouling, high energy requirements and high operating costs as limitations for nanofiltration/ultrafiltration; they also describe fouling, high operational cost and limitations at residual antibiotic concentrations for reverse osmosis. Avoid making a universal cost ranking across all membrane installations.

Adsorption transfers chemical substances from the liquid phase to the solid phase. It is therefore a separation/retention process, not proof of degradation. The loaded adsorbent and captured antibiotic require a recovery, regeneration and/or disposal strategy.

# Slide 5 — Fundamentals of Adsorption

### Learning objective

Explain the adsorbate–adsorbent system and the principal steps by which dissolved molecules reach adsorption sites.

### On-slide content

**Key terms**
- **Adsorbate:** dissolved antibiotic species being captured.
- **Adsorbent:** solid material providing the surface, pores and/or active sites.
- **Adsorption:** accumulation or retention at an interface.
- **Absorption:** uptake into the bulk of another phase.

**Principal steps described in the review**

Bulk water → movement toward the adsorbent → diffusion through the surrounding liquid film → movement toward accessible active sites → adsorbate–adsorbent interaction

*Pore filling is one possible adsorption mode, not an additional compulsory step.*

### Visual specification

Draw an original porous adsorbent particle with antibiotic molecules in bulk water, a liquid boundary film and arrows showing the four-step sequence: movement from bulk water toward the particle; diffusion across the liquid film; movement toward accessible active sites; and final interaction at the surface. Show pore filling as a separate, labelled callout (“possible retention mode”), not as a universal transport stage. Add a small inset contrasting surface accumulation with uptake into the material’s bulk.

### Presenter notes

Ajala et al. (2023) describe four principal steps: movement of adsorbate molecules from the bulk matrix toward the adsorbent, diffusion through the liquid film surrounding the adsorbent particle, movement toward active sites, and a final interaction with the adsorbent surface (PDF p. 9; journal p. 4686). The review's Figure 3 separately illustrates possible modes such as pore filling, electrostatic interaction, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interaction and surface complexation (PDF p. 16; journal p. 4693). Pore filling should therefore be shown as a possible mode, not inserted as a mandatory fifth transport step.

The four-step passage in Ajala et al. appears to contain the phrase “adsorbent–adsorbent interactions”. In context, the passage concerns antibiotic molecules (the adsorbate) reaching sites on the adsorbent; the slide therefore uses “adsorbate–adsorbent interaction” as an editorial clarification, not as a verbatim quotation.

The adsorption-versus-absorption distinction is standard chemistry teaching terminology. The inspected passages in the fixed papers do not provide a dedicated formal comparison of those terms. Rodríguez-López et al. (2024) separately distinguish adsorption and biosorption in terms of the sorbent's biological origin; this is a different distinction.

Stress that adsorption retains the antibiotic on a solid phase; this alone does not prove degradation or mineralisation.

### Source citations

- Ajala et al. (2023), PDF p. 9 / journal p. 4686 — four principal steps in the adsorption sequence.
- Ajala et al. (2023), Figure 3, PDF p. 16 / journal p. 4693 — illustrative adsorption modes, including pore filling.
- Rodríguez-López et al. (2024), PDF p. 2 — adsorption/biosorption distinction based on sorbent origin.

# Slide 8 — Factors Affecting Adsorption

### Learning objective

Explain how antibiotic speciation, adsorbent properties and water-matrix conditions influence adsorption, and why the effects are system-specific.

### On-slide content

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **pH** | Changes antibiotic speciation and adsorbent surface charge; their interaction can cause attraction or repulsion |
| **Adsorbent properties** | Surface charge, functional groups and accessible pore structure influence available sites and interactions |
| **Adsorbent dose** | Changes the amount of available adsorbent relative to pollutant; removal may approach a plateau |
| **Initial concentration and contact time** | Change the solute-to-site ratio and the time available to approach equilibrium |
| **Ionic strength / competing ions** | Different ions can alter charge effects or compete for adsorption sites |
| **Natural organic matter** | May compete with antibiotics for adsorption sites |
| **Temperature** | Can change equilibrium uptake; the direction and size of the effect depend on the system |

**Tetracycline example:** Gopal et al. (2020) report pKa values of approximately 3.3, 7.7 and 9.7. The dominant species changes progressively as pH increases.

*Experimental caution:* Check for precipitation before interpreting disappearance from solution as adsorption. In one ciprofloxacin–activated-biochar study, much of the apparent removal at pH 7–9 was precipitation.

### Visual specification

Left: reproduce an original, simplified TC speciation band based on Gopal et al. (2020), with pKa markers at 3.3, 7.7 and 9.7. Show progressive deprotonation: cationic at low pH, zwitterionic/intermediate forms, and anionic forms at alkaline pH. Mark pKa3 at 9.7 as further deprotonation in the alkaline range; do not imply that all speciation changes occur at one sharp boundary.

Add a small separate inset showing that **antibiotic pKa** and **adsorbent pHₚzc** are different properties. Right: a factor wheel around an adsorbent particle, with separate labels for ions and natural organic matter. Add a small “experimental caution” note: apparent CPX removal at pH 7–9 in Meseguer et al. (2024) was mainly precipitation, not adsorption.

### Presenter notes

pH affects both the ionic form of the antibiotic and the charge of the adsorbent. These properties must be considered together. Antibiotic pKa values describe acid–base dissociation of the molecule; the adsorbent's point of zero charge (pHₚzc) describes the pH at which its net surface charge is zero. They are not interchangeable.

**Tetracycline speciation.** Gopal et al. (2020) report TC pKa values of approximately 3.3, 7.7 and 9.7 and provide a species-distribution diagram (PDF pp. 2–3; journal pp. 27082–27083). Their simplified text describes cationic TC below pH 3.3, zwitterionic TC between pH 3.3 and 7.7, and anionic TC above pH 7.7. Retain the third pKa at 9.7 as a further alkaline deprotonation; do not omit it or imply a single sharp switch in the full distribution.

**Different antibiotics and adsorbents have different pH behaviour.** Perelomov et al. (2025) report OTC pKa values of 3.57, 7.49 and 9.88 and discuss a maximum kaolinite adsorption capacity near pH 5.5 for that particular OTC–kaolinite system (PDF p. 16). In Liang et al. (2024), the NFO/BC pHₚzc was approximately 5.35; TC removal was highest (92.4%) at pH 7.0 under the tested conditions (PDF p. 7). Do not transfer either optimum to other adsorbent–antibiotic pairs.

**Precipitation must be separated from adsorption.** Meseguer et al. (2024) report that CPX was predominantly zwitterionic between pH 5.9 and 8.9 and had low solubility in that form. In their activated-bagasse-biochar study, turbidity and blank tests showed that apparent removal at pH 7–9 was mainly caused by CPX precipitation rather than adsorption (PDF p. 8). The authors used pH 3.5 in subsequent tests to minimise precipitation. They report a pHₚzc of approximately 8.1 for the activated biochar (PDF p. 7). At pH 3.5, CPX is cationic while the biochar surface is positively charged; the authors therefore state that electrostatic contribution should be slight and suggest hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions as possible contributors (PDF p. 7). Do not present the pH 7–9 disappearance as adsorption performance.

**Dose and matrix effects.** In the NFO/BC–TC study, increasing dose from 0.2 to 1.0 g L⁻¹ raised removal from 37% to 93.9%; further increase gave little additional benefit, while adsorbent cost increased (Liang et al., 2024, PDF p. 7). Increasing humic-acid dosage reduced TC removal. NaCl had no significant effect in the reported tests, whereas MgCl₂ and CaCl₂ reduced capacity; at the reported 0.5% concentration, capacity fell to 59.8% and 63.8% of the original value, respectively (PDF p. 8). Perelomov et al. (2025) also report decreasing tetracycline adsorption with increasing ionic strength and reduced OTC sorption onto clay in the presence of high dissolved organic matter (PDF p. 16). These findings show that salt identity, matrix composition and adsorbent type matter.

**Temperature and contact time are also system-dependent.** For CIP on K₂CO₃-activated bagasse biochar, equilibrium uptake increased from 34.6 mg g⁻¹ at 10 °C to 109.9 mg g⁻¹ at 55 °C (Meseguer et al., 2024, PDF p. 8). About 82% of equilibrium uptake was reached after 24 h, with equilibrium at approximately 80 h under the stated conditions (PDF pp. 1 and 5). This is one experimental system, not a universal temperature or contact-time rule.

Initial concentration changes the solute-to-site ratio and is varied in the selected adsorption experiments. Particle size is reported as a preparation/operating parameter in Rodríguez-López et al. (2024, PDF p. 2), but that cited passage does not establish a universal directional effect of particle size on antibiotic uptake.

Do not claim that increasing dose, temperature, time or ionic strength always improves adsorption. Report the material, antibiotic and conditions whenever giving a directional trend.

- Gopal et al. (2020), PDF pp. 2–3 — TC pKa values and species-distribution diagram.
- Perelomov et al. (2025), PDF pp. 5–7 and p. 16 — clay surface properties, OTC pKa/pH example and ionic-strength/dissolved-organic-matter effects.
- Rodríguez-López et al. (2024), PDF p. 2 — particle-size preparation and operational parameters.

# Slide 9 — Adsorption Capacity and Removal Efficiency

### Learning objective

Calculate the mass of antibiotic retained per gram of adsorbent and the percentage removed from water.

### On-slide content

**Equilibrium adsorption capacity — constant-volume batch system**

\[
q_e=\frac{(C_0-C_e)V}{m}
\]

**Removal efficiency**

\[
Y(\%)=\frac{C_0-C_e}{C_0}\times100
\]

Where \(C_0\) and \(C_e\) are initial and equilibrium concentrations (mg L⁻¹), \(V\) is solution volume (L), \(m\) is adsorbent mass (g), and \(q_e\) is uptake (mg g⁻¹).

**Worked example — Mirizadeh et al. (2023), tetracycline on raw date-palm fibre**
- Conditions: pH 10; 298 K; contact time 3 h
- \(C_0=50\) mg L⁻¹
- \(V=30\) mL; dose = 1 g L⁻¹; therefore \(m=0.030\) g
- Reported \(q_e=16.15\) mg g⁻¹
- Derived \(C_e=33.85\) mg L⁻¹
- Derived \(Y=32.3\%\)

*Back-calculation note:* \(C_e=33.85\) mg L⁻¹ and \(Y=32.3\%\) are reconstructed from the reported \(q_e\) and dose. Mirizadeh et al. (2023), Figure 1, also plots removal efficiency for the pH series, but the article text does not state an exact numerical \(Y\) value for pH 10.

### Visual specification

Left: known solution + known adsorbent mass → controlled conditions → solid/liquid separation → residual concentration measurement. Right: equations and symbol definitions. Bottom strip labels each number “reported” or “derived”.

### Presenter notes

Capacity and removal percentage describe different things. Capacity is the amount retained per mass of adsorbent; removal percentage is the fractional decrease in liquid concentration. High percentage removal does not necessarily mean high capacity per gram.

Mirizadeh et al. (2023) report the TC uptake of 16.15 mg g⁻¹ at \(C_0=50\) mg L⁻¹, dose 1 g L⁻¹, volume 30 mL, pH 10 and 298 K, with 3 h contact time (PDF pp. 2–3). The dose and volume imply \(m=0.030\) g. The adsorbed mass is \(16.15\times0.030=0.4845\) mg. The concentration decrease is \(0.4845/0.030=16.15\) mg L⁻¹, so derived \(C_e=33.85\) mg L⁻¹ and derived removal \(=(50-33.85)/50\times100=32.3\%\).

Use \(q_e\) only for uptake at equilibrium; at a specified earlier time, use \(q_t\). Meseguer et al. (2024) use a volume-aware mass balance, \(q_t=(C_0V_0-C_tV_t)/m\), when liquid volume changes during sampling. Zhao et al. (2024) prints \((C_e-C_0)\) in its capacity/removal equations despite defining \(C_0\) as initial and \(C_e\) as equilibrium concentration; do not reproduce that sign as the positive uptake convention.

- Mirizadeh et al. (2023), PDF p. 3, Figure 1 and Section 3.1 — reported uptake and conditions.

# Slide 10 — Adsorption Isotherms

### Learning objective

Explain what an adsorption isotherm tells us, what the four common models assume, and how to interpret their fitted parameters without confusing a model fit with a proven mechanism.

### On-slide content

An **adsorption isotherm** is an equilibrium relationship measured at a specified temperature and solution condition. It shows how much antibiotic remains attached to the adsorbent as the dissolved concentration left in the water changes.

- \(C_e\): equilibrium concentration still dissolved in the water (mg L⁻¹).
- \(q_e\): equilibrium amount retained per gram of adsorbent (mg g⁻¹).

In a graph, \(C_e\) is usually on the horizontal axis and \(q_e\) on the vertical axis. Each point represents an equilibrium measurement.

**Langmuir — finite, idealised monolayer**

\[
q_e=\frac{q_{mL}K_LC_e}{1+K_LC_e}
\]

Assumes a limited number of equivalent adsorption sites. As these sites become occupied, uptake approaches a plateau.

- \(q_{mL}\): fitted limiting capacity (mg g⁻¹).
- \(K_L\): Langmuir affinity/equilibrium constant (L mg⁻¹).

**Freundlich — empirical, heterogeneous surface**

\[
q_e=K_FC_e^{1/n}
\]

Describes adsorption on a non-uniform surface, where sites can differ in their adsorption energies. It has no explicit finite saturation-capacity term.

- \(K_F\): Freundlich coefficient; its units depend on the exponent and concentration units.
- \(n\): exponent parameter controlling the curvature/intensity of the fitted relationship.

**Temkin — coverage-dependent adsorption heat**

\[
q_e=\frac{RT}{b_T}\ln(K_TC_e)
\]

Accounts for adsorbate–adsorbate interactions and assumes that the heat of adsorption decreases linearly as surface coverage increases.

- \(R\): gas constant; \(T\): absolute temperature.
- \(b_T\): parameter related to heat of sorption.
- \(K_T\): Temkin equilibrium constant.

**Sips — heterogeneous surface with finite saturation**

\[
q_e=\frac{q_{mS}K_SC_e^{1/n_S}}
{1+K_SC_e^{1/n_S}}
\]

Combines Freundlich-like behaviour at low concentration with Langmuir-like saturation at high concentration.

- \(q_{mS}\): fitted limiting capacity (mg g⁻¹).
- \(K_S\): Sips constant; units depend on \(n_S\).
- \(n_S\): dimensionless exponent associated with surface heterogeneity.

**Examples: the best fit depends on the antibiotic and adsorbent.**
- Mirizadeh et al. (2023): Langmuir best for TC; D–R best for CIP.
- Egbedina et al. (2021): model selection varies across CIP/TET and KCB material treatments.
- Meseguer et al. (2024): Freundlich best for CIP on activated bagasse biochar (ARE = 2.44%).

*Important:* A fitted model describes the measured equilibrium data. It does not, by itself, prove the molecular adsorption mechanism.

### Visual specification

Top: a small equilibrium schematic: dissolved antibiotic at concentration \(C_e\) ⇌ antibiotic retained on adsorbent, expressed as \(q_e\). Include the axes \(C_e\) (horizontal) and \(q_e\) (vertical).

Below: four compact model panels. Use schematic curves and label them as conceptual, not experimental data:
- Langmuir: rises then approaches a clear plateau.
- Freundlich: rises on a heterogeneous surface; do not draw a forced finite plateau.
- Temkin: show the model's logarithmic relationship over its applicable intermediate range.
- Sips: heterogeneous low-concentration behaviour that approaches a finite plateau.

At the bottom, add a compact comparison strip with the three study examples. Use the message: “Best fit is system- and condition-specific.”

### Presenter notes

**What an isotherm tells us.** An isotherm is made by allowing a series of adsorption experiments to reach equilibrium, then plotting the amount retained per mass of adsorbent against the concentration left in solution. The horizontal variable \(C_e\) is what remains dissolved after equilibrium; the vertical variable \(q_e\) is what has been taken up per gram of solid. A higher \(q_e\) at a given \(C_e\) means greater uptake under those stated conditions. Temperature, pH, antibiotic speciation, adsorbent dose and the concentration range all matter. It is not a rate curve: the isotherm does not tell us how long equilibrium takes to establish.

**Langmuir.** The model imagines a surface with a finite number of adsorption sites. In its idealised interpretation, those sites are equivalent and each site accommodates one adsorbed molecule, producing a monolayer. At low concentration, many sites are unoccupied and uptake rises with concentration. As concentration increases, more sites become occupied; eventually the curve approaches the limiting fitted capacity \(q_{mL}\). \(K_L\) describes the model's affinity/equilibrium response to concentration: a larger value generally gives a steeper rise under the same units and model form. These are model assumptions and fitted parameters, not direct proof that a real biochar surface is perfectly uniform.

Mirizadeh et al. (2023) report Langmuir as the best tested model for TC (\(R^2=0.977\)) and D–R as best for CIP (\(R^2=0.975\)); the authors interpret the TC Langmuir fit using the idealised homogeneous-site/monolayer description (PDF pp. 6–7). Do not infer that the surface has been microscopically proven to contain identical sites.

**Freundlich.** This is an empirical model used to describe heterogeneous surfaces: adsorption sites may differ in their energies or affinities. The exponent \(1/n\) controls how strongly uptake changes with dissolved concentration and therefore the curvature of the fitted line. \(K_F\) is a coefficient in that relationship; it is not a maximum capacity. Unlike Langmuir and Sips, the equation has no explicit finite saturation term. Do not extrapolate a Freundlich curve indefinitely and treat the resulting values as a physical unlimited capacity.

The selected studies do not use a fully uniform convention for the Freundlich exponent. Mirizadeh et al. (2023) report \(n=0.307\) for TC and \(n=0.384\) for CIP (PDF p. 7), whereas Meseguer et al. (2024) report \(n=6.44\) and interpret \(n>1\) as favourable under their stated convention (PDF p. 12); Egbedina et al. (2021) also uses \(n>1\) as a favourability indicator (PDF p. 7). Check the exact equation before interpreting or comparing \(n\); do not apply a universal threshold to raw values from different papers.

**Temkin.** The model accounts for indirect interactions between adsorbed molecules. Its central assumption is that the heat of adsorption decreases linearly as surface coverage increases. In plain language, the energetic benefit of adding more adsorbate is represented as changing with how much of the surface is already covered. The model is identified by Ayawei et al. (2017) as applicable over an intermediate range of ion concentrations (PDF p. 3). \(b_T\) is related to heat of sorption; \(K_T\) is the model equilibrium constant. Do not infer that adsorption is endothermic or exothermic from a Temkin fit alone. Mirizadeh et al. (2023) explicitly state that the Temkin model alone is insufficient to establish that thermodynamic direction (PDF p. 6); use separate thermodynamic analysis.

**Sips.** This model combines features of Freundlich and Langmuir. At low concentration, its exponent allows heterogeneous, Freundlich-like behaviour. At high concentration, the denominator allows the curve to approach the finite fitted limit \(q_{mS}\), like Langmuir. It is therefore useful when the data show surface heterogeneity but also approach saturation. \(n_S\) controls the heterogeneity-related curvature; \(K_S\) is the model constant. The fitted \(q_{mS}\) is not automatically an experimentally achieved uptake.

The Sips equation shown follows the parameterisation in Meseguer et al. (2024), Table 2 (PDF p. 6). Ayawei et al. (2017), Eq. (21) (PDF p. 4), prints a different denominator sign/parameterisation. Preserve this source discrepancy; do not claim the displayed expression is a verbatim transcription of both sources.

**Comparing actual studies.**
- Mirizadeh et al. (2023): Langmuir best for TC; D–R best for CIP (PDF pp. 6–7).
- Egbedina et al. (2021): among two-parameter models, CIP fits vary from Temkin on KCB to Langmuir on KCB-A/KCB-B; TET fits vary between D–R and Langmuir depending on material treatment (PDF pp. 5–7). The supplementary Table S1 was not supplied, so do not add missing detailed three-parameter values.
- Meseguer et al. (2024): nonlinear fits identify Freundlich as best for CIP on activated bagasse biochar by the lowest average relative error (ARE = 2.44%). The experimental curve approaches about 110 mg g⁻¹ but does not reach a clear plateau in the tested concentration range (PDF pp. 11–12).

**How to judge a fit.** Ayawei et al. (2017) explain that linearising an isotherm changes the error structure and recommend nonlinear regression on the original equation; model selection should not rely on \(R^2\) alone (PDF pp. 7–8). Meseguer et al. (2024) provide an example using nonlinear fitting and ARE. Compare candidate models within the same dataset, inspect residuals where available, report the fitting method and error criterion, and ensure parameters are physically meaningful. Do not compare an \(R^2\) from one paper directly with an ARE from another.

# Slide 11 — Adsorption Kinetics

### Learning objective

Explain how adsorption uptake changes with contact time, understand the meaning of the four kinetic models and their parameters, and recognise the limits of mechanistic interpretation.

### On-slide content

**Kinetics describes how quickly uptake develops with contact time.**

- \(q_t\): amount adsorbed per gram at time \(t\).
- \(q_e\): uptake at equilibrium (often fitted by the model).
- \(t\): contact time; use the same time unit as the fitted rate constant.

**Pseudo-first-order (PFO)**

\[
q_t=q_e\left(1-e^{-k_1t}\right)
\]

The rate is proportional to the remaining uptake capacity, \(q_e-q_t\). \(k_1\) controls how quickly the curve approaches \(q_e\) (time⁻¹).

**Pseudo-second-order (PSO)**

\[
q_t=\frac{k_2q_e^2t}{1+k_2q_et}
\]

The rate is proportional to the square of the remaining uptake capacity. \(k_2\) is the fitted rate constant (commonly g mg⁻¹ time⁻¹).

**Weber–Morris intraparticle diffusion**

\[
q_t=k_{\mathrm{WM}}t^{1/2}+C
\]

Relates uptake to the square root of time. \(k_{\mathrm{WM}}\) is the line's slope; \(C\) is its intercept, associated with boundary-layer contribution.

**Elovich**

\[
q_t=\frac{1}{\beta}\ln(1+\alpha\beta t)
\]

Represents a rate that decreases as surface coverage develops. \(\alpha\) is the initial-rate parameter; \(\beta\) controls how the rate declines.

*PFO and PSO are empirical “pseudo” rate models. A better fit alone does not prove a particular molecular mechanism.*

**Contact time differs across the selected studies**
- Mirizadeh et al. (2023): about 120 min for TC and 90 min for CIP on raw date-palm fibre.
- Egbedina et al. (2021): most uptake in the first 15 min for the tested hybrid biochars.
- Meseguer et al. (2024): more than 3 days to reach equilibrium for CIP on activated bagasse biochar.

*These are not head-to-head results; materials and experimental conditions differ.*

### Visual specification

Top: an illustrative \(q_t\)-versus-time curve rising rapidly at first and then approaching a plateau. Label it “schematic”; mark \(q_e\) as the equilibrium uptake and \(t\) on the horizontal axis.

Middle: four model cards with the equation, one-sentence meaning and key parameter definitions. Distinguish the Weber–Morris plot, where the horizontal variable is \(t^{1/2}\), from the other time-based model curves.

Bottom: show the transport sequence: bulk liquid → liquid boundary film → external adsorbent surface → pores → internal adsorption sites. Add a caution: “The model with the best fit does not automatically identify the rate-controlling mechanism.”

### Presenter notes

**What kinetics tells us.** An adsorption kinetic experiment follows the uptake at successive contact times. The quantity \(q_t\) is the amount retained per gram at a particular time; \(q_e\) is the uptake after the system has reached equilibrium. A kinetic curve therefore answers “how fast does uptake develop?” An isotherm, by contrast, relates equilibrium uptake to equilibrium concentration and does not describe the time required to reach equilibrium.

For constant liquid volume, time-dependent uptake is \(q_t=(C_0-C_t)V/m\). If the liquid volume changes during sampling, use the volume-aware mass balance. Vareda (2023) describes bulk transport, liquid-film diffusion, pore diffusion and surface interaction/reaction as relevant steps. The slowest relevant step may control the overall observed rate (PDF p. 2).

**Why the models are called “pseudo”.** Vareda (2023) explains that PFO and PSO originate from reaction-kinetic equations developed for homogeneous systems but are adapted to describe uptake on a heterogeneous solid. The “pseudo” designation reflects, among other assumptions, treating the dissolved adsorbate concentration as approximately constant and expressing the rate in terms of solid-phase uptake rather than liquid-phase concentration (PDF p. 2). These mathematical forms are useful for describing uptake curves, but they should not be read as direct proof of the molecular reaction order or surface chemistry.

**Pseudo-first-order (PFO).** The differential form is \(dq_t/dt=k_1(q_e-q_t)\). In plain language, the model makes the uptake rate proportional to the gap between the fitted equilibrium uptake and the uptake reached so far. At the beginning, this gap is large and uptake can be rapid; as \(q_t\) approaches \(q_e\), the gap narrows and the predicted rate slows. \(k_1\) has units of inverse time; a larger fitted \(k_1\) means the model approaches its fitted equilibrium uptake more quickly under the same time convention. Vareda (2023) explains that PFO can be derived under surface-reaction assumptions but can also match diffusion-related behaviour, particularly near equilibrium. A PFO fit therefore does not uniquely diagnose the rate-controlling step (PDF pp. 5–6).

**Pseudo-second-order (PSO).** The differential form is \(dq_t/dt=k_2(q_e-q_t)^2\). Here the rate is proportional to the square of the remaining uptake gap. The integrated equation predicts a curve that rises and then approaches the fitted \(q_e\). With \(q\) in mg g⁻¹ and time in minutes, \(k_2\) is commonly expressed in g mg⁻¹ min⁻¹. Its numerical value depends on the uptake and time units and on the operating conditions; do not compare raw \(k_2\) values across studies without matching conventions and conditions. Vareda (2023) shows that PSO may fit both surface-reaction-controlled and diffusion-controlled data. A better PSO fit alone does not prove chemisorption (PDF pp. 3–5).

**Weber–Morris intraparticle-diffusion model.** This model relates \(q_t\) to \(t^{1/2}\). In a plot of uptake against square-root time, the slope \(k_{\mathrm{WM}}\) is the apparent intraparticle-diffusion rate parameter and the intercept is associated with boundary-layer contribution. The slide uses \(C\) for the intercept; Vareda (2023) writes the corresponding constant as \(E\), while the selected experimental papers use \(C\) or step-specific \(C_i\). More than one linear region can indicate that the uptake curve contains multiple stages. A line passing through the origin is consistent with intraparticle diffusion being the sole rate-limiting step under the model assumptions; a nonzero intercept or multiple regions indicates that intraparticle diffusion alone does not describe the whole process. The intercept is an empirical indicator, not a direct measurement of boundary-layer thickness (Vareda, 2023, PDF p. 6).

**Elovich model.** This model describes a rate that declines as surface coverage develops. In the integrated equation, \(\alpha\) is the initial adsorption-rate parameter and \(\beta\) is the Elovich parameter associated with the decrease in rate as uptake proceeds. Vareda (2023) discusses Elovich in the context of chemisorption kinetics on heterogeneous surfaces, but notes that the model neglects simultaneous desorption and can behave non-physically at long times. The review therefore limits its use to conditions far from equilibrium. A good Elovich fit alone is not independent proof of chemisorption (PDF p. 6).

**What the selected antibiotic studies report.**
- Mirizadeh et al. (2023) compare PFO, PSO and Weber–Morris for TC and CIP on raw date-palm fibre. PSO gives higher reported \(R^2\) and lower residual standard deviation than PFO for both antibiotics; the authors interpret this as chemisorption, but Vareda's methodological review means this must be reported as the authors' interpretation, not as proof from PSO alone (PDF pp. 5–6). Their Weber–Morris plots contain multiple linear regions, consistent with multistep uptake (PDF pp. 5–6).
- Egbedina et al. (2021) report that most CIP and TET uptake on the tested hybrid biochars occurs within the first 15 min. Their preferred PFO/PSO assignment varies by antibiotic and material treatment (PDF p. 7). The full kinetic parameter table is in missing supplementary Table S2; do not invent those values.
- Meseguer et al. (2024) report that more than 3 days are required to reach equilibrium for CIP on activated bagasse biochar under their stated conditions (PDF p. 9). Their five-model comparison gives Elovich the lowest ARE (2.52%), followed by Bangham (3.17%); this is a statistical ranking, not proof of a unique mechanism (PDF p. 10). Their Weber–Morris plot has two linear zones, with the first associated with boundary-layer transport and the second with diffusion through micropores and mesopores (PDF pp. 10–11).

**Model fitting and interpretation.** Vareda (2023) explains that linearising kinetic equations can bias model selection toward PSO and distort fitted parameters; in a simulated PFO dataset, linear regression selected PSO as the better fit. The review recommends nonlinear regression on the original equations and use of criteria such as AIC and BIC, for which lower values are preferred. It cautions against relying on \(R^2\) alone for nonlinear fits and against including post-equilibrium data points in kinetic fitting (PDF pp. 7–9). Plot the predicted curve against the original \(q_t\)-versus-time data and inspect residuals where available.

- Vareda (2023), PDF pp. 3–6 — PSO, PFO, Weber–Morris and Elovich equations, assumptions and limitations.
- Vareda (2023), PDF pp. 7–9 — linearisation bias, nonlinear regression and AIC/BIC.

# Slide 12 — Thermodynamics of Adsorption

### Learning objective

Explain what Gibbs free energy, enthalpy and entropy tell us about adsorption, read the Van't Hoff relationship, and distinguish thermodynamic favourability from adsorption rate and capacity.

### On-slide content

**Three quantities answer three related questions**

- **Gibbs free-energy change, \(\Delta G\):** Is adsorption thermodynamically favourable under the thermodynamic convention used by the authors?
- **Enthalpy change, \(\Delta H\):** Is heat absorbed or released during adsorption?
- **Entropy change, \(\Delta S\):** How does the net dispersal/number of accessible arrangements change for the defined system?

**Gibbs relationship reported by Liang et al. (2024)**

\[
\Delta G=-RT\ln K_d
\]

**Gibbs–Helmholtz relationship**

\[
\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S
\]

The Gibbs free-energy change combines the enthalpy and entropy contributions at temperature \(T\).

**Van't Hoff relationship reported by Liang et al. (2024)**

\[
\ln K_d=-\frac{\Delta H}{RT}
+\frac{\Delta S}{R}
\]

For a plot of \(\ln K_d\) against \(1/T\):

- Slope \(=-\Delta H/R\)
- Intercept \(=\Delta S/R\)

**Sign interpretation**

| Quantity | Sign | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| \(\Delta G\) | Negative | Thermodynamically favourable under the stated convention |
| \(\Delta H\) | Positive | Endothermic — heat absorbed |
| \(\Delta H\) | Negative | Exothermic — heat released |
| \(\Delta S\) | Positive / negative | Net entropy increases / decreases |

**Temperature-response example:** For CIP on K₂CO₃-activated bagasse biochar, reported \(q_e\) increased from 34.6 to 109.9 mg g⁻¹ between 10 and 55 °C.

*Favourability is not speed. An endothermic trend or a negative \(\Delta G\) does not, by itself, prove a molecular mechanism.*

### Visual specification

Left: three connected definition cards—\(\Delta G\) (favourability), \(\Delta H\) (heat), and \(\Delta S\) (entropy change)—with plus/minus interpretations.

Right: a **schematic** Van't Hoff plot of \(\ln K_d\) versus \(1/T\), with slope \(=-\Delta H/R\) and intercept \(=\Delta S/R\). Label it “relationship, not experimental data”. Include a small note that the plotted equilibrium quantity must be consistently defined and dimensionless for a thermodynamic logarithm.

Bottom: four-point temperature-response plot from Meseguer et al. (2024), showing the reported CIP uptake values at 10, 25, 40 and 55 °C. Label the adsorbent and conditions.

### Presenter notes

**What thermodynamics tells us—and what it does not.** Thermodynamics describes the energy balance and equilibrium favourability of adsorption under defined conditions. Kinetics describes how quickly uptake changes with time. Capacity describes how much is retained. These questions are related, but they are not interchangeable: a process can be thermodynamically favourable and still be slow; a large adsorption capacity does not by itself establish a favourable free-energy change.

**Gibbs free-energy change, \(\Delta G\).** Gibbs free energy combines the enthalpy and entropy contributions at a specified temperature. Under the standard-state convention used in a thermodynamic analysis, a negative \(\Delta G\) is interpreted as favourable/spontaneous, while a positive value is unfavourable/non-spontaneous. The standard-state symbol matters: the sign should be interpreted using the study's defined equilibrium quantity and reference states. A negative value does not mean that adsorption happens instantly, and it does not identify whether the molecular interaction is electrostatic, hydrogen bonding, complexation or another mechanism.

**Enthalpy change, \(\Delta H\).** Enthalpy change describes the heat effect associated with the adsorption process. A positive value means the process is endothermic: heat is absorbed. A negative value means it is exothermic: heat is released. This is an energy/heat statement, not a rate statement. A positive \(\Delta H\) alone does not prove chemisorption. Some papers use enthalpy-magnitude thresholds to classify adsorption as physical or chemical; present those thresholds as that paper's interpretation, not as universal diagnostic boundaries.

**Entropy change, \(\Delta S\).** Entropy is related to the number of accessible microscopic arrangements and the dispersal of energy/matter in the defined system. A positive entropy change means the net entropy increases under the convention and system boundary used; a negative value means it decreases. In adsorption, solute molecules becoming associated with a solid surface can reduce freedom in one part of the system, while release or rearrangement of water and ions can contribute in the opposite direction. The sign is therefore a net thermodynamic result; avoid simplifying it to “the adsorbent surface becomes more disordered” without evidence. Liang et al. (2024) describe the entropy change for NFO/BC as positive, but the numerical value is in the unavailable Table S4 (PDF p. 10).

**How the three terms work together.** The relationship \(\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S\) shows that favourability depends on both the heat effect and the entropy contribution, and on temperature. For an endothermic process (positive \(\Delta H\)), a sufficiently favourable positive entropy contribution can help make \(\Delta G\) negative at higher temperature. This is a general explanation of the equation, not a claim that every endothermic adsorption process behaves identically.

**Van't Hoff plot.** Liang et al. (2024) report \(\ln K_d=-\Delta H/(RT)+\Delta S/R\). It has the linear form \(y=mx+c\) if \(y=\ln K_d\) and \(x=1/T\): the slope is \(-\Delta H/R\), and the intercept is \(\Delta S/R\). Temperature must be in kelvin. If \(R=8.314\;\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\), enthalpy must be expressed in J mol⁻¹ and entropy in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, or all terms must be converted consistently to kJ units.

**Source-symbol note.** Liang et al. (2024) describe these as standard thermodynamic parameters in the text, but their printed Eqs. (5)–(7) use \(\Delta G\), \(\Delta H\) and \(\Delta S\) without a superscript degree symbol. The equations on this slide retain the paper's printed notation rather than silently adding \(^{\circ}\).

In the equations, \(R\) is the gas constant, \(T\) is absolute temperature (K), and \(K_d\) is the equilibrium/distribution quantity used by Liang et al.; its exact operational definition and standard-state normalisation are not clear in the supplied main text.

**Important equilibrium-constant limitation.** Liang et al. (2024) print \(\Delta G=-RT\ln K_d\) and the Van't Hoff equation using \(K_d\) (PDF pp. 9–10). However, the inspected main text does not clearly state its operational definition or standard-state normalisation, and Table S4 is absent from the supplied PDF. The argument of a thermodynamic logarithm must be dimensionless. Therefore, reproduce the equations as the authors' reported relationships, but do not independently calculate or reconstruct their numerical \(\Delta G\), \(\Delta H\) or \(\Delta S\) from an undefined or dimensioned coefficient. This is an evidence limitation; it is not sufficient to conclude that the authors' calculations are necessarily invalid.

**Evidence from Liang et al. (2024).** For TC adsorption on sawdust biochar (BC), nickel ferrite (NFO) and NFO/BC, the authors report negative \(\Delta G\) values for all three materials and interpret adsorption as spontaneous under their conditions. They report that adsorption on NFO is exothermic, whereas adsorption on BC and NFO/BC is endothermic. The main text explicitly gives \(\Delta H=+31.384\;\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) for NFO/BC and describes its \(\Delta S\) as positive (PDF p. 10). The paper uses a threshold of 20.9 kJ mol⁻¹ to interpret the process as chemically dominated; present that as the authors' interpretation, not a universal rule. Numerical values from Table S4 must not be invented.

**Temperature-response example from Meseguer et al. (2024).** For CIP on K₂CO₃-activated bagasse biochar, reported uptake was 34.6, 41.4, 89.3 and 109.9 mg g⁻¹ at 10, 25, 40 and 55 °C, respectively (PDF p. 9). Conditions were pH 3.5, 0.025 g adsorbent, 50 mL of 300 mg L⁻¹ solution and 72 h contact time (PDF p. 5). The authors interpret the increase as endothermic behaviour. The main paper does not provide a numerical \(\Delta G\), \(\Delta H\) or \(\Delta S\) table; do not calculate these from the four uptake values alone.

**Other selected studies.**
- Mirizadeh et al. (2023) qualitatively report endothermic adsorption of TC and CIP on raw date-palm fibre, but the supporting information containing the detailed thermodynamic study was not supplied. Their paper explicitly cautions that the Temkin model alone cannot determine whether adsorption is endothermic or exothermic (PDF p. 6). Do not infer heat effect from the Temkin parameter alone.
- Zhao et al. (2024) report increasing temperature-dependent adsorption capacity and describe the process as endothermic (PDF p. 11). Their temperature-dependent values at 298, 308 and 318 K are Langmuir-fitted \(q_m\) values (PDF p. 13), not direct thermodynamic parameters. Do not convert the fitted-capacity trend into a numerical enthalpy.
- Chen et al. (2024) mention thermodynamic experiments in the methods, but the supplied main PDF does not provide accessible numerical \(\Delta G\), \(\Delta H\) or \(\Delta S\) results. Do not infer them from the kinetic or isotherm data.

**Cross-study caution.** The studies do not show that all antibiotic adsorption is endothermic. Liang et al. (2024) report opposite heat effects for NFO versus BC and NFO/BC. Mirizadeh, Meseguer and Zhao report endothermic trends for their respective tested systems. These observations are material- and antibiotic-specific and cannot be collapsed into one universal rule.

# Slide 13 — Regeneration and Reusability

### Learning objective

Explain how a spent adsorbent is treated for reuse and distinguish antibiotic release from restored adsorption performance.

### On-slide content

**A regeneration and reuse cycle**

Adsorption → separate the loaded solid → desorb antibiotic into a regenerant → rinse / neutralise / dry → test the next adsorption cycle

**Four terms that should not be confused**

- **Desorption:** release of adsorbed antibiotic from the solid into a liquid regenerant.
- **Regeneration:** treatment of the spent adsorbent to restore usable adsorption function; it may include desorption, rinsing, neutralisation and drying.
- **Reuse:** placing the treated adsorbent into a new adsorption run.
- **Capacity retention:** capacity after reuse relative to the capacity of the fresh adsorbent.

\[
\text{Capacity retention (\%)}=
\frac{q_n}{q_{\text{fresh}}}\times100
\]

Here, \(q_n\) is the adsorption capacity measured after cycle \(n\), and \(q_{\text{fresh}}\) is the capacity of the fresh adsorbent, measured on the same basis and under comparable conditions.

**Selected repeated-cycle evidence**

| Study | Regeneration procedure | Reported result |
|---|---|---|
| Chen et al. (2024): OTC and CIP on magnetic cotton-husk biochar | Ethanol + ultrasound; rinse and dry | After 5 cycles: 86.8% OTC and 91.1% CIP capacity retained |
| Liang et al. (2024): TC on NiFe₂O₄/biochar | DI-water desorption in the reusability test | 72% TC removal after cycle 4—not a reported capacity-retention percentage |
| Zhao et al. (2024): TC on fermentation-residue biochar | 0.1 M NaOH; rinse to pH 7 and dry | 5 cycles; the authors describe a slight capacity decrease, without stating an exact final retention percentage in the text |

**A cycle-removal percentage is not the same as capacity retention.**

### Visual specification

Use a circular process diagram: adsorption → solid/liquid separation → desorption into a regenerant stream → rinse/neutralise/dry → next adsorption test. Show the antibiotic-containing regenerant as a separate liquid stream that requires recovery or appropriate treatment. Beside it, place the three-study table. Highlight Chen et al.'s result as capacity retention; label Liang et al.'s 72% explicitly as removal after cycle 4; label Zhao et al.'s outcome as a qualitative capacity trend, not a numerical retention value.

### Presenter notes

**What each term means**

Desorption is the step in which antibiotic already held by the adsorbent is released into a liquid regenerant or eluent. The antibiotic has moved from the solid into another liquid stream; it has not necessarily been destroyed.

Regeneration is the treatment of a spent adsorbent to make it useful for another adsorption run. Depending on the system, it may involve desorption followed by washing, neutralisation, drying or another treatment. Desorption can be one part of regeneration, but the terms are not interchangeable.

Reuse means that the treated adsorbent is actually placed into a new adsorption experiment or treatment cycle. To evaluate regeneration performance, the next adsorption run must be measured. Gkika et al. (2025) distinguish desorption efficiency from regeneration (removal) efficiency: a desorption test measures how much contaminant is released, whereas adsorption performance in the subsequent cycle shows whether the regenerated solid is still effective (PDF p. 7).

**Capacity retention versus removal**

Capacity retention compares the adsorption capacity after reuse with that of the fresh adsorbent:

\[
\text{Capacity retention (\%)}=
\frac{q_n}{q_{\text{fresh}}}\times100
\]

The comparison is meaningful only when both capacities are measured using the same calculation basis and comparable conditions, such as antibiotic, initial concentration, pH, adsorbent dose, temperature and contact time.

Removal efficiency answers a different question: what fraction of the antibiotic initially present in the liquid was removed during that particular adsorption run? A percentage removal measured in cycle four does not by itself say what fraction of the fresh adsorbent's original capacity remains. This is why Liang et al.'s 72% TC removal after cycle four must not be rewritten as 72% capacity retention.

**What the selected experimental papers report**

Chen et al. (2024) regenerated magnetic cotton-husk biochar composite (Fe₂O₃@BMBC) using ethanol plus ultrasound. The procedure used 0.5 L ethanol with 50 mg adsorbent, sonicated at 500 W for 60 min, followed by 0.5 L ultrapure-water washing and drying at 70 °C. The reuse test used 50 mg adsorbent, 20 mL antibiotic solution at an initial concentration of 500 mg L⁻¹, 25 °C and 24 h adsorption (PDF pp. 3–4). After five reuse cycles, the authors report 86.8% of fresh OTC capacity and 91.1% of fresh CIP capacity remaining (PDF p. 10; Fig. 7, PDF p. 11). The water-washing control retained only 21.2% and 19.7%, respectively. These are capacity-retention values, not desorption efficiencies.

The stated 0.5 L ethanol and 0.5 L rinse-water volumes for 50 mg adsorbent each correspond to 10 L of liquid per gram of adsorbent. This is a direct unit conversion from the reported laboratory protocol, not a process-cost or scale-up estimate. It flags the need to account for regenerant and wash-water demand. The article also reports Fe leaching below 0.4 mg L⁻¹ over the tested pH range of 3–11 (PDF p. 10); this does not replace a full mass balance on adsorbent loss or the treatment of the spent regenerant.

Liang et al. (2024) tested tetracycline adsorption–desorption for four cycles. In the reusability subsection, the authors state that the adsorbent was recovered by centrifugation and desorbed with deionized water before being returned to TC solution. They report a TC removal rate of 72% after the fourth cycle (PDF p. 8; Fig. 4f). The result is a cycle-specific removal rate, not an explicit percentage of fresh adsorption capacity retained. The methods section also mentions washing with DI water and ethanol and drying at 60 °C (PDF p. 4), but the role of ethanol in the cycle protocol is not fully clarified in the main text; the slide therefore describes the reusability subsection's DI-water desorption without implying a fully specified combined protocol.

Zhao et al. (2024) conducted five consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles for OSOB-3-700. The stated conditions were 0.25 g L⁻¹ adsorbent, 250 mg L⁻¹ initial TC concentration, 180 rpm and 24 h. Regeneration used 0.1 M NaOH, followed by deionized-water rinsing to pH 7 and drying at 80 °C (PDF p. 5). The authors state that capacity decreased slightly across the five cycles (PDF p. 13); Figure 10 plots both removal rate and adsorption capacity over the cycles (PDF p. 14). The main-text conclusion does not provide a precise final capacity-retention percentage, so none is inferred from the plotted lines.

**Desorption is not automatically successful regeneration**

Rodríguez-López et al. (2024) measure antibiotic sorption and desorption/release from hemp waste, oak ash and mussel shell, but do not report a repeated adsorption–desorption reuse series. For CIP at the highest initial concentration tested, the reported single-desorption percentages were 22.4% for hemp waste, 16.4% for oak ash and 15.5% for mussel shell (PDF p. 5). These data describe release from the tested solids; they do not demonstrate that the solids recovered their original adsorption performance. Their stirred-flow experiments also show that antibiotics can be released from by-products, so remobilisation and the fate of the released antibiotic matter.

Meseguer et al. (2024) identify regeneration and reuse tests as further work needed to establish the practical applicability of their activated bagasse biochar; the supplied main paper does not report completed reuse cycles (PDF p. 13).

Regeneration creates a secondary liquid stream containing desorbed antibiotic and regenerant. Eluent demand, wash-water demand, ultrasound or other energy use, treatment time, drying, adsorbent loss, leaching and disposal or recovery of the spent regenerant all affect practical feasibility. The different studies use different antibiotics, adsorbents, procedures and performance metrics; their cycle results should not be directly ranked.

# Slide 14 — Low-Cost Potential and Practical Challenges

### Learning objective

Evaluate whether a waste-derived adsorbent is cost-effective across the whole treatment process.

### On-slide content

**The real cost chain**

Feedstock → preparation → activation/modification → adsorption operation → recovery → regeneration → residual-waste management

**Cost drivers**
- Feedstock collection, transport, storage and drying
- Reagents, pyrolysis, inert gas, milling and synthesis
- Adsorbent dose, contact time, mixing and separation
- Eluent, washing, drying and solvent recovery
- Treatment of antibiotic-loaded liquids and spent solids

**Practical questions**
- Does it work in real wastewater?
- Can the solid be recovered efficiently?
- Does capacity remain useful after regeneration?
- What is the total cost per m³ treated?

**Evidence gap:** The selected antibiotic studies do not provide comparable full production costs or costs per volume of antibiotic wastewater treated.

### Visual specification

A process-cost pipeline with a “hidden costs” band: energy | chemicals | water | recovery | regeneration | waste handling. Include the distinction: €/kg adsorbent ≠ €/m³ treated water.

### Presenter notes

Waste-derived feedstock does not automatically mean a low-cost treatment process. Kyzas and Kostoglou (2014) identifies equipment, adsorbent consumption, electricity, regeneration/reactivation and spent-adsorbent disposal as cost elements; it warns that a low-cost adsorbent that is difficult to regenerate may not be economical (PDF pp. 19–20).

The antibiotic studies describe process requirements but do not provide comparable full cost calculations. Meseguer et al. (2024) includes drying, argon pyrolysis, K₂CO₃ activation, an 800 °C treatment, washing and further drying (PDF pp. 2–4). Zhao et al. (2024) use high-moisture fermentation residue, KOH activation, nitrogen pyrolysis, acid washing, water rinsing and vacuum drying (PDF pp. 2–4). Chen et al. (2024)'s magnetic composite requires high-temperature pyrolysis, iron precursor and ball milling; its ethanol/ultrasound regeneration uses substantial liquid volumes relative to adsorbent mass (PDF pp. 3–4). Liang et al. (2024) use nickel and iron salts, ammonia adjustment, hydrothermal processing, centrifugation and repeated washing (PDF pp. 3–4).

Gkika et al. (2025) provide a useful cost-accounting framework and numerical examples, but many cases concern non-antibiotic pollutants such as ibuprofen and BPA. Do not present these figures as antibiotic-treatment costs. The source also contains an unresolved difference between one Table 5 cost entry and the summary range in its conclusion.

A cost per kilogram of adsorbent cannot be directly compared with a cost per litre or cubic metre of treated water. Treatment cost depends on dose, influent and target concentrations, working capacity, recovery and regeneration. Meseguer et al. (2024) explicitly calls for real-effluent and regeneration/reuse work (PDF p. 13).

### Learning objective

### On-slide content

**Key conclusions**

1. Antibiotic adsorption depends on antibiotic speciation, adsorbent surface chemistry, pore structure and water-matrix conditions.
